1. Introduction
The Republic of Moldova is a candidate country for accession to the European Union and has committed to
harmonizing its national legislation with the requirements of the EU acquis and ensuring its subsequent
implementation. As a candidate country for EU accession, the Republic of Moldova is required to establish a
functional integrated waste management system. This objective is considered one of the key priorities of the
European Union accession process.
One of the most important waste streams under EU law is packaging and packaging waste, which has been
regulated since 1994 through Directive 94/62/EC. The Directive introduced obligations concerning the
prevention, reuse, recovery and recycling of packaging waste, as well as requirements for the establishment
of return, collection and recovery systems, based on the “polluter pays” principle and on the responsibility of
the economic operators involved. Over time, this framework has evolved into a more detailed and harmonised
extended producer responsibility regime, strengthened through subsequent amendments to EU legislation
and, most recently, through Regulation (EU) 2025/40 on packaging and packaging waste, which replaces
Directive 94/62/EC and introduces more ambitious requirements that are directly applicable in the Member
States.
Packaging includes all products made from any type of material—paper and cardboard, plastics, glass, metal,
or wood—used to contain, protect, handle, deliver, and present goods, from raw materials to finished products.
Once their function has been fulfilled and they are discarded, packaging becomes packaging waste. The
presence of packaging in nearly all sectors of the economy—the food and beverage industry, retail, the
pharmaceutical sector, logistics, and consumer goods—makes it one of the largest and fastest-growing waste
streams globally. Per capita packaging consumption has risen steadily in recent decades, driven by the growth
of e-commerce, changing consumer habits, the expansion of ready-to-use and single-serving products, and
the globalization of supply chains.
Managing the ever-increasing volumes of packaging waste has become a global and European challenge,
driven by the high volume of materials placed on the market, the diversity of packaging materials and formats,
and the inadequacy of collection and sorting infrastructure in many countries. Packaging waste is not merely
a logistical problem but reflects a complex interplay of environmental, economic, and social factors. Large
quantities of packaging waste are not managed properly: materials are landfilled, incinerated without energy
recovery, or end up in the environment as litter, leading to significant losses of valuable secondary raw
materials such as paper fibres, glass, aluminium, steel, and various plastic polymers. This increases the
pressure on the extraction and production of new virgin raw materials, with the associated energy consumption
and indirect environmental impacts. Inadequate management of packaging waste leads to environmental
contamination—most visibly through plastic pollution of soils, waterways, and ecosystems—and poses risks
to human health and biodiversity.
Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)—mandatory for packaging waste management in the EU—is a public
policy approach whereby producers are responsible for managing the packaging they place on the market at
the end of its life cycle, including its collection, sorting, recycling, and recovery. EPR is essential for packaging
waste management because it shifts the financial burden from taxpayers to producers, encourages eco-design
and packaging reduction at the source, improves separate collection and recycling rates, and promotes the
circular economy. This instrument can support the Republic of Moldova in managing packaging waste in a
modern and environmentally sound manner.
The main objective of this study is to assess the amount of packaging placed on the national market in the
Republic of Moldova and to analyse the actual rates of waste collection, recovery, and recycling, with a view
to identifying existing shortcomings and recommending measures to align with EU requirements.
Moldova’s path to EU membership and the need for waste sector reform
The Republic of Moldova officially submitted its application for EU membership in March 2022 and obtained
candidate country status in June of the same year. This status entails specific obligations: the Republic of
Moldova must progressively transpose and implement the entire EU legislative package in the field of the
environment, including legislation governing packaging and packaging waste. Full implementation is expected
to be achieved by approximately 2035. Although this timeline provides for a transition period, it requires
sustained legislative action, institutional capacity building, infrastructure investments, and stakeholder
engagement—all of which must be initiated without delay. Early identification of implementation gaps and the
rapid development of corrective measures are therefore a strategic necessity.
The broader challenge of municipal waste management

The specific challenges regarding packaging waste management in the Republic of Moldova must be
understood within the broader context of the national municipal waste management system, which faces
significant structural difficulties, highlighted in this report to provide context for the findings presented. The
waste hierarchy—which prioritizes prevention, reuse, and recycling over disposal—remains largely
unenforced. Organized waste collection covers only a limited portion of the population, and a significant
proportion of collected municipal waste, including packaging waste, ends up in non-compliant landfills and
unauthorized disposal sites. Expenditures on waste management at all levels are insufficient to ensure the
protection of the environment and public health, and service fees in most municipalities do not allow for full
cost recovery. The absence of “pay-as-you-throw” pricing systems also removes any financial incentive for
households to reduce waste generation or to separate recyclable fractions at the source, including packaging.
These structural deficiencies directly affect packaging waste management. EPR schemes for packaging can
only function effectively when integrated into a broader system capable of supporting separate collection,
ensuring proper sorting and treatment, and enabling the enforcement of producer obligations. Where the
foundations of municipal waste management are weak, implementing extended producer responsibility
becomes considerably more difficult.
The importance of identifying implementation gaps
The Republic of Moldova has taken significant steps toward transposing the relevant EU directives, and the
legal framework for implementing EPR for packaging waste is in place. However, legal transposition is only
the first step—the key challenge lies in the gap between legal provisions and their practical application.
Identifying areas where the implementation of extended producer responsibility is insufficient, sectors where
collection and recycling rates remain inadequate, and areas where the institutional and financial architecture
requires strengthening is essential for developing effective corrective measures. The findings and
recommendations of this study aim to provide stakeholders with information and evidence—to the extent that
data availability permits—to prioritize reforms within the timeframe required by the EU accession process.
Structure of this report
To this end, the report includes an assessment of the legal and institutional framework, the physical systems
required for implementation, and the quantities of packaging placed on the market, collected, and recycled.
Chapter 2 presents the policy and legal frameworks applicable to waste management and includes a detailed
analysis of the regulatory framework regarding packaging and packaging waste at the EU level and in Moldova.
Chapter 3 assesses the implementation of this legal framework and producer responsibility in the Republic of
Moldova. Chapter 4 analyses the infrastructure and systems available for the collection, sorting, and treatment
of packaging waste, and assesses the quantities placed on the market, the corresponding targets set, as well
as the collection and recycling rates achieved. Chapter 5 presents the key conclusions of the study, and the
recommendations formulated based on the analysis.

2.1.1.   Overview of EU policies and legislation in the field of waste management

Policy objectives set out in the European Green Deal and the Circular Economy Action Plan
At the European Union level, waste management policy is part of a broader agenda for the transition to a
circular, resource-efficient, and climate-neutral economy. The European Green Deal1 sets the strategic
direction for this transition, aiming to transform the EU into a modern, competitive, and sustainable economy
where economic growth is decoupled from resource use and environmental impact is reduced. Within this
framework, waste management is no longer viewed merely as the final stage of a product’s life cycle, but as
part of an integrated policy on product design, waste prevention, reuse, repair, recycling, and keeping materials
in the economy for as long as possible.
A key instrument for implementing these objectives is the Circular Economy Action Plan2 , adopted in 2020
as one of the pillars of the European Green Deal. The plan covers the entire product life cycle, from design
and production through to consumption, collection, recycling, and the reintegration of secondary raw materials
into the economy. Its main objective is to make sustainable products the norm in the EU market, to reduce
pressure on natural resources, and to limit waste generation, particularly in sectors with a high environmental
impact, such as packaging, plastics, textiles, electrical and electronic equipment, batteries, vehicles, and
construction.
From the perspective of the waste sector, these policies strengthen the application of the waste hierarchy,
prioritizing prevention, reuse, and recycling over disposal. They also support the expansion of producer
responsibility, separate collection, improving the quality of recycled materials, developing markets for
secondary raw materials, and reducing landfilling. Thus, EU waste policy is evolving from a predominantly
reactive model, focused on managing waste that has already been generated, toward a preventive and circular
model, in which responsibility is distributed throughout the entire value chain—producers, consumers, public
authorities, and economic operators. This approach serves as the reference framework for legislative and
institutional reforms in Member States and in countries aligning their legislation with the EU acquis, including
in the areas of extended producer responsibility and specific waste streams.
Waste management in the EU: framework legislation, regulations on waste management operations,
specific requirements for materials
Overall, EU waste legislation combines general rules on the management of all waste with sector-specific and
material-specific requirements.
At the European Union level, the framework regulation in the field of waste is Directive 2008/98/EC on waste3
(the Waste Framework Directive). It establishes the basic principles and concepts applicable to waste
management, as well as the general principles guiding EU policy in this area.
A central element of EU legislation is the waste hierarchy, which establishes the order of priority for waste
management operations: waste prevention, preparation for reuse, recycling, other forms of recovery, including
energy recovery, and disposal as a last resort. This approach reflects the shift from a policy focused on waste

The Framework Directive also regulates aspects such as liability for waste management, operator

In addition to the framework legislation, the EU has developed specific regulations for waste management
operations, such as:

separate collection. The mandatory targets for Member States regarding the preparation for reuse and

    - by 2025, the preparation for reuse and recycling of municipal waste must increase to at least 55% by

    - by 2030, (...) it shall be increased to at least 60% by weight;
    - by 2035, (...) it will be increased to at least 65% by weight.

dedicated regulations on product design, separate collection, recycling targets, restrictions on hazardous
substances, recycled content, and producer responsibility. Sector-specific legislation establishes the principle
of extended producer responsibility (EPR) and requirements for EPR schemes through acts such as:
     -    Regulation (EU) 2025/40 on packaging and packaging waste (PPWR), which repeals the former
          Directive 94/62/EC on packaging and packaging waste (PPWD);
     -    Directive 2012/19/EU on waste electrical and electronic equipment (WEEE), currently under review;
     -    Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 on batteries and waste batteries (which repeals the former Directive
          2006/66/EC on batteries and accumulators, as well as waste batteries and accumulators);
     -    Directive 2000/53/EC on end-of-life vehicles (ELV), which will be replaced by the proposed Regulation
          on circularity requirements for vehicle design and end-of-life vehicle management4 .
For the identification and classification of waste, Decision 2000/532/EC on the European list of waste, as
amended by Decision 2014/955/EU, is relevant; this is an important regulation for the coding of waste streams
and for the definition of hazardous waste.
Directive (EU) 2019/9045 on the reduction of the impact of certain plastic products on the environment
(the SUP Directive) establishes obligations regarding the separate collection of certain plastic products that
fall under the category of packaging (Art. 9, para. (1)): “Member States shall take the necessary measures to
ensure the separate collection for recycling: (a) by 2025, of a quantity of single-use plastic products (...) equal
to 77%, by weight, of the single-use plastic products placed on the market in a given year; b) by 2029, a
quantity of single-use plastic products (...) equal to 90%, by weight, of the single-use plastic products placed
on the market in a given year.”
Such products listed in the SUP Directive, Annex Part F, include single-use plastic products such as “beverage
bottles with a capacity of up to three litters, including their caps and lids,” excluding glass or metal beverage
bottles with plastic caps and lids. According to the SUP Directive, to achieve this objective, Member States
may, among other things: a) establish deposit-refund schemes; b) set separate collection targets for the
relevant extended producer responsibility schemes.

Finally, we note the new Regulation (EU) 2024/1157 on waste shipments, which has been in effect at the

through stricter audit requirements and equivalent financial guarantees/insurance.
The Regulation applies to shipments between EU Member States, exports and imports to and from non-EU
countries, and waste transiting the EU.
For exports from the EU to non-EU countries, the export of waste from the EU is prohibited as a general
rule; the only exceptions apply to EFTA member countries; there are no other exceptions.

    -    hazardous waste and certain other types of waste (listed in Annex V of the Regulation), including

    -    non-hazardous waste is prohibited, with the exception of: metal and metal-containing waste subject to

         can demonstrate that the waste will be managed in an environmentally sound manner and requests
         inclusion on the list of approved destinations that the Commission will adopt by November 21, 2026,

For imports into the EU from non-EU countries, the Regulation prohibits imports of waste intended for
disposal or recovery, except for waste originating from Parties to the Basel Convention or other countries with
which the EU or its Member States have concluded bilateral or multilateral agreements, as well as in other
exceptional situations (e.g., crisis, war).
The Regulation requires competent authorities in the EU to ensure that imported waste does not endanger
human health and is managed in an environmentally sound manner. Furthermore, all parties involved in the
recovery or disposal of waste must apply good environmental practices throughout the entire process, as
defined in the Regulation. Regarding the obligations of Member States, the Regulation imposes specific
monitoring requirements and enforcement measures, requiring them to: inspect sites, facilities, brokers,
dealers, transport operations, and treated material; develop and implement national inspection plans; impose
appropriate penalties for violations of the Regulation; ensure coordination among their own enforcement
agencies; cooperate with other Parties to the Basel Convention.
Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)
Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) is one of the central instruments of environmental policy at the
European Union level, and its importance has grown significantly following the adoption of the European Green
Deal and the Circular Economy Action Plan. These documents redefine Europe’s economic growth and
sustainable development model, placing circularity and responsibility throughout the entire product life cycle
at the centre of the development strategy.
Conceptually, EPR was initially formulated as a public policy principle aimed at reducing environmental impact
across a product’s entire life cycle by extending the producer’s responsibility beyond the manufacturing stage
to include collection, recycling, and final disposal. This approach was legally grounded in the “polluter pays”
principle.

Directive 2008/98/EC on waste (Framework Directive) defines an “extended producer responsibility scheme”
as “a set of measures taken by Member States to ensure that producers of products bear financial or financial
and organizational responsibility for the management of the waste stage of a product’s life cycle” (Art. 3, point
21).
According to the assessment of EPR schemes at the EU level, the most visible impact of their implementation
has been an increase in recycling rates, a result of the quantitative targets imposed by EU and national
legislation. Given the marked heterogeneity among countries in how EPR schemes are defined and
implemented, with varying levels of efficiency and performance, the EU has in recent years initiated a process
of legislative reform at the EU level, focused on harmonizing certain elements of the legislation on EPR
schemes.
Thus, the 2018 revision of the Framework Directive, through Directive (EU) 2018/851, includes and details a
minimum set of general requirements applicable to EPR schemes (Articles 8 and 8a).
The minimum general requirements for the implementation of EPR systems at the Member State level (Art. 8)
require Member States to ensure, at the national level, the implementation of an extended producer
responsibility regime for all products placed on the market, and for all producers. The Directive provides
examples of the types of measures through which this obligation can be fulfilled: acceptance of returned
products and generated waste, waste management, and financial responsibility for these activities, as well as
information obligations regarding recyclability and reuse.
As of 2018, following the amendments introduced by Directive (EU) 2018/85, the Framework Directive requires
Member States to apply the general minimum requirements set out in Article 8a where, among the measures
establishing the extended producer responsibility system, the establishment of extended producer
responsibility schemes (EPR schemes) is included. These minimum requirements outline the obligations
applicable to producers/collective systems, as well as the obligations applicable to Member States regarding
their responsibilities to incorporate the minimum requirements into the design and implementation of EPR
schemes through national legislation.
2.1.2.   The new EU legal framework on packaging and packaging waste

Regulation (EU) 2025/407 on packaging and packaging waste (abbreviated as PPWR) entered into force on
February 11, 2025, and applies from August 12, 2026. Directive 94/62/EC8 is repealed as of the date of entry
into force of the Regulation, except for certain provisions that remain in force until the dates expressly specified
in the Regulation for each provision, but no later than January 1, 2030.
The Regulation establishes requirements regarding the sustainability and labelling of packaging throughout its
life cycle, including production, use, and waste management. The new regulatory framework applies to all
packaging and packaging waste, regardless of the material from which they are made or their origin (industrial,
commercial, household, etc.), complementing existing EU legislation on waste management.
It aims to:
    -    preventing the use of unnecessary packaging and promoting reuse, refilling, and recycling;
    -    harmonizing national measures to avoid barriers to trade and distortions of competition;
    -    contributing to the circular economy and achieving climate neutrality by 2050.
Packaging that complies with the provisions of the regulation may be freely marketed throughout the EU. EU
Member States may not impose additional national requirements that conflict with the regulation, unless the
regulation explicitly provides for or permits this.
Packaging must be designed to minimize the presence of harmful substances, protecting human health and
the environment. All packaging must be recyclable, which means it must:
    -    be designed for material recycling;
    -    be capable of being collected, sorted, and recycled on a large scale when it becomes waste—the
         requirement for packaging to be recycled on a large scale takes effect in 2035.
The Regulation establishes a system of performance classes (A, B, C) regarding recyclability, applicable from
2030, detailed in Annex II, Table 3, and introduces stricter obligations regarding recyclability starting in 2038
(Art. 6(3)). Packaging may only be placed on the market if it falls within classes A, B, or C as of January 1,

As elements of sustainability and circularity, the Regulation introduces several mandatory requirements for
producers, among which we mention those that would have an impact on how waste is managed within
collective systems:
    -   Compostable packaging must comply with industrial composting standards. Member States may
        require compostability for certain types of packaging if adequate infrastructure exists.
    -   Operators introducing reusable packaging must ensure that a system is in place for its collection and
        reuse, must participate in reuse systems, and must ensure that the packaging is reconditioned before
        reuse—a new type of collective system is established, designed to manage reusable packaging. By
        2030, beverage distributors must ensure that 10% of products are packaged in reusable containers,
        reaching 40% by 2040.
    -   Operators offering refill options must inform consumers about the types of containers they can use,
        hygiene standards, and safety regulations. Starting in 2030, large retailers must strive to allocate 10%
        of their sales floor space to refill stations.
    -   By 2030, 40% of transport packaging must be reusable, reaching an ambitious target of 70% by 2040;
        and, by 2030, 10% of group packaging must be reusable, reaching an ambitious target of 25% by
        2040.

regulation for EPR systems, they are summarized below.
The reuse system
The Regulation defines both the “reuse system” and the “system operator,” particularly in Article 3(31) and
Annex VI (Specific requirements for reuse systems and refill stations), establishing a new and more detailed
legal framework for the reuse of packaging. In conjunction with the obligation under Article 29(6), beverage
distributors must ensure that 10% of products are packaged in reusable containers by 2030, rising to 40% by
2040. The Regulation establishes harmonized requirements for classifying packaging as “reusable,” and the
Commission will subsequently establish, through delegated acts, other requirements, such as a minimum
number of cycles for reusable packaging (Article 11).
The reuse system is the set of organizational, technical, or financial arrangements, together with the associated
incentives, that enable the reuse of packaging in a closed or open system; the regulation includes as an
example a deposit-return system that ensures the collection of packaging for reuse (Art. 3(31)).

The system operator is defined as any natural or legal person who is a participant in the system and who
manages the reuse system (Annex VI). Annex VI also defines the system participant, that is, the entity that
may collect packaging, recondition it, distribute it, transport it, fill it, package it, or provide it to end users. Annex
VI, Part B, defines the requirements for reconditioning, an essential process for restoring reusable packaging
to a functional state for the purpose of its reuse.
The Regulation contributes to advancing the ambition regarding reusable packaging, requires that such
packaging be managed within a reuse system for certain streams, and sets reuse targets, such as for certain
transport packaging and, in some cases, for beverages, starting in 2030 (Art. 29).
Annex VI sets out specific requirements for these systems: governance requirements, fair access for economic
operators, collection and return rules, incentives for return, the distribution of costs and benefits, how the
system’s operation will be monitored, and the existence of a system operator to verify its proper functioning.
According to Art. 52, Member States shall take measures to encourage the establishment of packaging reuse
systems that include sufficient incentives for return and environmentally sound refill systems. Such measures
may include, but are not limited to: the use of deposit-refund systems; the use of economic incentives, including
obligations imposed on final distributors to charge fees for the use of single-use packaging and to inform
consumers about the cost of such packaging at the point of sale; requiring manufacturers or final distributors
to make a certain percentage of products available in reusable packaging through a reuse system or by refilling.
Reducing the generation of packaging waste
Chapter VIII on the management of packaging and packaging waste includes references to how Member
States may use EPR schemes and organizations to achieve targets for reducing packaging waste generation.
Member States are required to reduce per capita packaging waste, compared to per capita packaging waste
generated in 2018, by at least: 5% by 2030; 10% by 2035; 15% by 2040. The Commission will adopt rules
establishing a correction factor to account for the impact of tourism on waste generation (Art. 43).
Article 43(5) of the Regulation extends the role of EPR beyond the financing of waste management, allowing
Member States to use EPR schemes as a mechanism to prevent the generation of packaging waste, including
through “the use of economic instruments and other measures to provide incentives for the application of the
waste hierarchy,” and by requiring producers or EPR implementation organizations to adopt waste prevention
plans. The use of EPR for this purpose must comply with the principles of proportionality and non-
discrimination, as well as the requirement not to create trade barriers or distortions of competition. At the same
time, the measures must not encourage the mere replacement of packaging with lighter alternatives if this
does not actually lead to the prevention of waste generation.
Extended Producer Responsibility
The Regulation stipulates that producers are responsible for the entire life cycle of the packaging they place
on the market, including the management of the resulting waste.
The Regulation contains detailed rules on the register of producers and extended producer responsibility in
Articles 44–47 (Chapter VIII, Section 2). Member States are required to establish a national register of
producers to monitor compliance with the Regulation’s requirements by producers (Article 44(1)), within 18
months of the Commission’s adoption of an implementing act that will establish “the format for registration and
reporting to the register and specify the granularity of the data to be provided and the types of packaging and
categories of materials that must be covered by the information submitted” (Art. 43(14)). This harmonized
format will be interoperable, based on open standards, machine-readable, and transferable via an
interoperable, vendor-neutral data exchange network—a development that confirms the trend toward greater
harmonization and standardization already evident in recent amendments to EU waste legislation.
The Regulation requires producers to register in the register of each Member State where they first place
packaging or packaged products on the market, an obligation that may be fulfilled, under certain conditions,
through the EPR implementation organization designated by the producer or, if Member States permit, through
an authorized representative for the REP, designated by written mandate from the producer. The application
for registration is also harmonized, comprising at least the information detailed in Annex IX, and Member States
may request additional information or documents if necessary to monitor compliance with the obligations
established by the Regulation.
The requirements for reporting data on the types and quantities of packaging placed on the market and the
performance of management are detailed in Art. 44(7)-(10) and Annex IX, Part B.
The producer/authorized representative/EPR organization shall submit the information specified in Annex IX,
Part B, point 1, annually by June 1. This essentially covers the basic data necessary to identify the producer

other states or third countries, as well as the quantities collected separately for certain single-use beverage

Similar to the EPR regulation for other waste streams, the regulation sets out a series of obligations for the
competent authority managing the national register, such as registration deadlines, a prohibition on adding
substantive requirements for registration, the possibility of charging proportional, cost-based fees, and
requirements regarding the publication and accessibility of the list of registered producers (easily accessible,
free of charge, searchable, machine-readable, compliant with open standards, etc.).
According to Article 45 on Extended Producer Responsibility, under EPR schemes established in accordance

    -   the costs of labelling waste containers for the collection of packaging waste (cf. Art. 13; these are the

    -   the costs of conducting surveys on the composition of mixed municipal waste.

or validate the information regarding:

    -   a self-certification from the producer confirming that it offers only packaging for which EPR
        requirements are met in the Member State where the consumer is located.
The producer is responsible for the accuracy of the information provided.
Article 46 details the requirements for organizations implementing EPR. It provides that producers may
entrust or transfer their obligations under the Regulation to an EPR organization, but allows Member States to
adopt measures making participation in the collective system mandatory for producers (i.e., requiring
producers to join an EPR implementation organization).
The Regulation requires Member States to ensure that the entire national territory is covered by authorized
EPR systems and to establish an independent mechanism (independent third party) or a competent
public authority to oversee the coordinated fulfilment of obligations by EPR organizations.
EPR organizations are required to publish annually on their websites data on the quantities of packaging
placed on the market and the system’s performance regarding the recovery and recycling of materials from
such packaging, in addition to the mandatory information that must be published pursuant to Art. 8a of Directive
2008/98/EC (minimum general requirements for EPR).
Art. 55 establishes the obligation to inform end-users about their role in preventing packaging waste,
including best practices, methods of reuse, the role of end-users in separate collection, the meaning of labels
and symbols visible on packaging, the environmental impact of improper disposal, the management of
compostable packaging, etc. The information must be up-to-date and provided through accessible means,
such as websites, information campaigns, and educational programs, and must be clearly communicated. The
information obligations apply to EPR systems starting August 12, 2028.
Regarding the authorization requirements for EPR systems (for individual or collective fulfilment of
obligations), these are detailed in Article 47. The Regulation sets out the general requirements for
authorization, and Member States, through their competent authorities, establish detailed requirements and
authorization procedures. The Regulation imposes the following as minimum requirements to be

    -   the authorization procedure, including the timeframe for review, shall not exceed 18 weeks from the

        packaging waste for consumers, adapted to the quantity and types of packaging placed on the market;

Requirements regarding return and collection systems, as well as mandatory collection, are regulated in

Article 48 requires Member States to establish return, separate collection, and sorting systems for packaging
waste, with nationwide coverage, non-discriminatory access, and sufficient capacity, in order to facilitate high-
quality reuse and recycling. Under certain conditions, Member States may derogate from separate collection
for certain streams, provided this does not affect preparation for reuse, recycling, or other recovery operations
and does not reduce the quality of the results. Requirements for sorting are set forth to extract packaging
designed for recycling, and the incineration or landfilling of packaging designed for recycling is prohibited.
Return and collection systems must be open to all relevant actors, cover the entire national territory, include
all types of packaging waste, and be accessible to end-users, including in public, commercial, and residential
areas. Member States may involve public waste management systems in the organization of these systems.
Deposit-Return Schemes (DRS)
According to Article 50, DRSs become mandatory for Member States for certain types of packaging. They shall
take the necessary measures to ensure that deposit-return systems are established for the specified packaging
formats and that a deposit is collected at the point of sale. DRS are necessary to achieve the target of separate
collection of at least 90% per year, by weight, by January 1, 2029, for the following packaging formats:
    -   single-use plastic beverage bottles with a capacity of up to three litters; and
    -   single-use metal beverage containers with a capacity of up to three litters.
The regulation provides for the possibility of exemptions for the HORECA sector, under specified conditions
(the product is consumed on-site, and the packaging is collected at that location). Possible exceptions are also
provided for certain categories of beverages and packaging (wine or wine-like products, spirits, milk, and dairy
products), but these may be included in the DRS by Member States. Finally, Member States may be exempted
from the obligation to introduce a deposit-return system if they demonstrate a high level of separate collection
already achieved (at least 80% according to data reported for the year 2026) and present a credible plan for
achieving the 90% target; if collection performance falls below the required threshold, the exemption is
withdrawn, and the deposit-return system must be established within a specified timeframe.
Member States are encouraged to extend these systems to other types of beverage packaging, including
reusable packaging, where feasible. The Regulation requires that the return of reusable packaging be at least
as accessible/easy for users as the return of single-use packaging.
New deposit-return systems must comply with the minimum requirements in Annex X, and the Commission
will subsequently assess their implementation and interoperability. Existing deposit-return systems may be
temporarily exempted from the minimum requirements in Annex X if they meet the 90% target by 2029;
otherwise, they must be brought into line with the requirements of the annex by 2035 at the latest.
Conclusions
An analysis of the EPR provisions in Regulation (EU) 2025/40 confirms the trend toward harmonizing waste
management requirements (as well as broader product-related requirements, criteria/requirements for
products placed on the market, traceability documents, and evidence of compliance, verification, monitoring,
and reporting procedures, etc.), both through detailed rules contained in legislative acts and through provisions
that permit or require the adoption of subsequent harmonization acts (delegated or implementing acts). These
are adopted by the Commission on the basis of provisions expressly set forth in the basic regulation (the
legislative act); they are adopted through regulations, directives, or decisions; they are binding; and they bring
about a higher degree of harmonization through detailed rules such as uniform criteria/requirements,
calculation and reporting methodologies, standard reporting formats, and databases, etc.

2.2. The policy and legal framework for waste management in Moldova
2.2.1.   National public policy in the waste sector

Waste policy
The Environmental Strategy for 2024–2030, approved by Government Decision No. 409/2024, reaffirms the
importance of implementing extended producer responsibility mechanisms as an essential tool for developing
separate collection and recovery systems for specific waste streams, including hazardous waste. Under
Priority Area 6.3 “Implementation of Extended Producer Responsibility,” the document promotes the
development of collection and treatment infrastructure for waste streams such as waste electrical and
electronic equipment (WEEE), end-of-life vehicles, waste batteries and accumulators, used oils, and
packaging waste.
The strategy stipulates that the effective operation of EPR schemes must contribute to the development of
separate collection and resource recovery at the regional level, setting several targets for the period 2023–
2027 regarding the recycling and recovery of specific waste streams. These include recycling 25% of
packaging waste generated in rural areas and 35% in urban areas.
At the same time, the Strategy includes projections regarding the evolution of specific waste quantities for the
2023–2030 period. According to the Strategy, the estimated quantities of specific waste are projected to be
approximately 20.54 thousand tons in 2026, 20.35 thousand tons in 2027, 23.05 thousand tons in 2028, 22.84
thousand tons in 2029, and 22.64 thousand tons in 2030 (Table 15).
According to the Waste Management Strategy of the Republic of Moldova for 2013–2027, approved by
Government Decision No. 248/2013, the worsening of the waste problem, particularly regarding municipal solid
waste, is caused by inadequate management of the various stages of the waste collection, transport,
treatment, and disposal of waste. The Strategy highlights that the existing waste management system is
characterized by underdeveloped infrastructure, limited coverage of sanitation services, a lack of separate
collection, and reduced recycling and recovery capacities.
The document emphasizes that municipal waste generation is influenced by several socio-economic factors,
including the population’s income level, consumer behaviour, urbanization, and the increase in consumption
of packaged products. At the same time, the Strategy notes that the expansion of packaging use and the
increase in the quantities of packaged products placed on the market contribute significantly to the rise in the
volume of waste generated and to changes in its composition.
The Strategy also acknowledges that the lack of adequate infrastructure for separate collection and waste
treatment leads to the disposal of significant quantities of recyclable materials along with mixed waste, thereby
reducing the potential for reuse and recycling. In this context, the document promotes the application of the
waste hierarchy, with an emphasis on waste prevention, reuse, recycling, and recovery, in accordance with
the principles of EU waste management policy.
The National Waste Management Program for 2023–2027, approved by Government Decision No.
972/2023, is the main operational public policy document in the field of waste management and the circular
economy in the Republic of Moldova. The program was developed to implement the provisions of Law No.
209/2016 on waste and the commitments undertaken by the Republic of Moldova under the RM–EU
Association Agreement.
The program aims to develop an integrated waste management system that is economically efficient and
capable of ensuring the protection of the environment and public health. The document promotes the transition
from an approach focused predominantly on waste disposal to a model based on waste prevention, separate
collection, reuse, recycling, and material recovery, in accordance with the waste hierarchy applicable at the
European Union level.
To this end, the Program provides for:
    -    the development of regional waste management infrastructure;
    -    the creation of regional integrated waste management centres;
    -    the development of transfer stations and infrastructure for separate collection;
    -    the closure and rehabilitation of non-compliant landfills;
    -    the development of waste sorting, recycling, and recovery capacities.
Packaging waste is explicitly recognized in the Program as one of the priority waste streams for which extended
producer responsibility schemes are to be implemented, alongside WEEE, batteries and accumulators, used
oils, tires, and end-of-life vehicles. This recognition is important because it places extended producer

responsibility for packaging within the national public policy agenda and provides a basis for the further
development of the regulatory and operational framework.
However, the Program does not contain specific targets, deadlines, or measures dedicated to packaging for
the separate collection and recycling of packaging waste. The provisions relevant to packaging remain largely
general, being integrated into broader infrastructure development objectives, such as the expansion of
separate collection points and the development of sorting capacities, which will serve packaging waste
alongside other waste streams.
The program assigns an important role to strengthening monitoring, reporting, and control mechanisms, as
well as to developing the institutional capacities of public authorities and stakeholders involved in waste
management—both of which are prerequisites for the functioning of an effective EPR system for packaging.
Waste sector legislation
Law No. 209/2016 on waste is the framework law in the field of waste management and partially transposes
the provisions of Directive 2008/98/EC on waste. The law establishes the legal and institutional foundations of
national waste management policy, with the objective of preventing or reducing the negative effects generated
by waste generation and management, mitigating the impact of natural resource use, and increasing the
efficiency of their use, in the context of the transition to a circular economy.
The law establishes the fundamental principles applicable in the field of waste management, including the
“polluter pays” principle, the waste hierarchy, extended producer responsibility, waste prevention, and the
efficient use of resources. At the same time, it sets out the obligations of public authorities, producers, and
economic operators involved in waste management, as well as the general framework for implementing
systems for separate collection, recycling, recovery, and reporting of waste.
The law provides the legal basis for the adoption of sector-specific regulations applicable to specific waste
streams subject to the EPR regime, including packaging waste, WEEE, batteries and accumulators, end-of-
life tires, used oils, and end-of-life vehicles.
Extended producer responsibility and regulations on specific waste streams
To implement the principle of extended producer responsibility, as provided for in Law No. 209/2016 on waste,
and to progressively align with the European Union acquis in the field of waste management, the Republic of
Moldova has adopted a series of sectoral regulations applicable to priority waste streams.
The Regulation on Packaging and Packaging Waste, approved by Government Decision No. 561/2020,
establishes the legal framework for the management of packaging and packaging waste, including obligations
regarding waste prevention, reuse, recycling, and recovery of packaging. The Regulation aims to ensure a
high level of environmental protection and the effective functioning of extended producer responsibility
mechanisms for packaging.
At the same time, the Instruction on the Keeping of Records and the Transmission of Data and Information on
Waste and Its Management, approved by Government Decision No. 501/2018, establishes obligations
regarding the recording, reporting, and transmission of data on waste generation and management, including
for operators involved in the implementation of extended producer responsibility schemes.
Packaging deposit system (SDA)
One of the most significant recent developments in the field of packaging waste management is the adoption
of Government Decision No. 379/2025 on the establishment of the Packaging Deposit System (SDA). The
regulation establishes the legal framework and mechanism for the creation and operation, at the national level,
of a single deposit system applicable to reusable packaging and single-use packaging for which a return
guarantee is established, in accordance with the provisions of Article 54¹ of Law No. 209/2016 on waste.
The SDA is the mechanism through which producers fulfil their extended producer responsibility obligations
regarding the collection, transport, reuse, and recycling of packaging included in the system. The functions of
the SDA administrator are to be performed by a legal entity designated by the Ministry of the Environment, in
accordance with the procedure established by regulation and by Law No. 209/2016.
The system applies to certain categories of beverages sold in single-use glass, plastic, and metal containers
with volumes ranging from 0.1 to 3 litters, including water, soft drinks, beer, cider, juices, and other alcoholic
and non-alcoholic beverages.
According to the legal framework, starting January 1, 2027, producers’ obligations regarding the collection,
transport, reuse, and recycling of packaging and packaging waste covered by the SDA will be fulfilled
exclusively through the deposit system.

Law on the authorization of activities in the waste management sector
Activities in the waste management sector are subject to the authorization regime provided for by Law No.
160/2011 on the regulation of entrepreneurial activity through authorization, as well as sector-specific
legislation in the field of waste management and environmental protection. Depending on the type of activity
carried out, economic operators must obtain permits such as an environmental permit for waste management,
an integrated environmental permit, health permits, or other permits provided for by applicable legislation.
The permitting regime applies to activities involving the collection, transport, treatment, recovery, recycling,
and disposal of waste, including operators involved in implementing extended producer responsibility
obligations. At the same time, the regulatory framework establishes obligations regarding waste traceability,
data reporting, and compliance with the technical and environmental conditions applicable to waste
management facilities and activities.
In practice, industry stakeholders have reported difficulties related to authorization procedures, including the
length of administrative processes, a lack of clarity regarding certain recycling and treatment activities, and the
existence of operators conducting activities without the necessary authorizations.

recovery of waste, in accordance with the principles of resource efficiency and the transition to a circular

These objectives constitute the main strategic benchmarks for the development of waste management
infrastructure, the implementation of EPR schemes, and the strengthening of separate collection and recycling
mechanisms.

2.2.2.    Relevant fiscal and environmental policies

Relevant environmental policies and framework legislation
Law No. 178/2025 on public sanitation services (in force as of July 21, 2026) establishes the legal and
institutional framework for the organization, management, and operation of public sanitation services in the
Republic of Moldova. The law regulates the powers of local public administration authorities, the rights and
obligations of sanitation operators, as well as the principles for organizing and providing municipal waste
collection, transport, and management services.
The law promotes the expansion of separate municipal waste collection, the improvement of sanitation service
coverage, and the development of the infrastructure necessary for waste collection, transfer, and treatment.
At the same time, the law establishes the general framework for cooperation between local public
administration authorities and operators involved in waste management, including in the context of
implementing EPR systems and developing complementary separate collection systems for waste streams
regulated by EPR schemes.
Fiscal and financial instruments
Law No. 1540/1998 on payments for environmental pollution establishes the legal framework and
economic mechanisms applicable to the collection of payments for the environmental impact generated by
certain activities and products. The law regulates, among other things, payments for pollutant emissions, waste
disposal, and the tax on goods that, during use, cause environmental pollution.
In the context of implementing EPR schemes, Law No. 1540/1998 is particularly relevant, as it establishes
fiscal mechanisms applicable to certain categories of products and packaging placed on the market. Currently,
the law includes a mechanism for reducing or exempting the payment of the packaging tax, applicable
depending on the degree to which packaging waste recovery and recycling targets are met.
Thus, if recovery targets through recycling are met, economic operators may benefit from the corresponding
reduction or exemption from the tax applicable to plastic, paper and cardboard, glass, wood, and metal
packaging, including aluminium.
At the same time, the Ministry of the Environment has submitted for public consultation the draft of the new

2.3. National legal framework on packaging and packaging waste
2.3.1.   Relevant requirements for the implementation of EPR under Law No. 209/2016 on waste

Regarding the role of EPR in the waste management system, Law No. 209/2016 on waste10 establishes the
legal pillars of the extended producer responsibility system, defining the categories of products subject to EPR,
producers’ obligations regarding the organization and financing of the collection, treatment, recovery, and
recycling of waste from products placed on the market, as well as the operational framework for individual and
collective systems.
Through its provisions on EPR, the law creates the institutional and operational framework for implementation,
as well as the institutional mechanisms for authorization, monitoring, control, and reporting, thereby
establishing the legal basis for the development of public waste management infrastructure and for the
organization and operation of EPR systems. The specific implementation mechanism for each product stream
subject to the EPR regime and the waste generated by them is established through regulations approved by
the Government (EPR Regulations on WEEE, BA/DBA, packaging, used tires, used oils, VSU, and, in the
future, textiles).
This section provides a summary of several general regulatory aspects applicable to the EPR regime.
General aspects regarding the organization and implementation of EPR
The EPR system applies to producers who design, manufacture, process, treat, and place on the market
products subject to EPR, and placing on the market also includes importation/release for free circulation. EPR
is defined as the set of financial and/or organizational obligations imposed on producers for the recovery,
recovery, or recycling of end-of-life products or products that have become waste (Art. 12(1), (2)).
One provision in the law concerns the option for producers to fulfil their EPR obligations through an
individual or collective system, with collective fulfilment (collective system) being mandatory for products
marketed for household consumption; for products intended for users other than households, it is permitted to
fulfil the responsibility through an individual e system (Art. 12(2) a) and b)). In the case of packaging, the EPR
Regulation specifies the criteria and restricts producers’ option for the individual system.
Producers must register with the EPR Producers List, keep records of products placed on the market for 5
years, and submit annual reports. At the time of registration in the List of Producers, they must provide proof
of an individual system (an adequate description of how they will fulfil their obligations and proof of the
existence of individual systems for free take-back, separate collection, treatment, and recovery) or certify their
membership in a collective system. It is prohibited to place on the market products from producers who are
not registered in the List of Producers. Furthermore, producers who do not comply with the registration
procedure bear the operational costs of waste management associated with the EPR for products placed on
the market from the entry into force of the EPR Regulations, corresponding to the annual obligations to meet
the minimum collection and recovery targets (Art. 29(4)1, letter c)).
Producers have obligations to organize and finance the separate collection system and, subsequently, to
manage the collected waste, including to meet the collection and recovery targets set forth in the legislation,
as well as the obligation to finance all these activities and to report to the Environmental Agency. In addition
to these costs, other types of costs that must be covered by producers’ financial contributions include: costs
related to informing waste holders, data collection and reporting, administrative expenses, and, in the case of
packaging, those related to implementing the deposit system.
The law stipulates that these financial contributions must be adjusted according to criteria that reflect the
reduced environmental impact of the products. We note that we did not find this provision regarding the
mandatory modulation of EPR fees (producers’ financial contributions) in the EPR Regulation on packaging

Self-monitoring mechanisms (censor, audit, supervisory body)
Collective systems are required to implement self-monitoring mechanisms, such as a supervisory body
represented by a censor, and periodic independent external audits, once every 3 years (Art. 12(2)(4)). The
auditor prepares an annual report on the collective system’s financial activities, and an external auditor will
conduct an audit of the collective system’s operations once every three years, including aspects related to the
quality of collected data and reported s (Art. 12(5)b3 )). The external audit report is submitted to both the
members of the collective system and the supervisory authorities, upon request.
To monitor compliance with contractual obligations (cf. contracts concluded with local public authorities for the
coverage of EPR waste from municipal waste and with authorized operators for the collection of EPR waste
generated from the non-municipal waste stream in all localities), the collective system shall establish the
supervisory body provided for in Art. 121 (6), composed of an equal number of representatives of the
collective system and of the associations of authorized operators, as well as a representative of the Congress
of Local Authorities in Moldova (CALM) and a representative of the Ministry of the Environment. From
discussions with representatives of the collective systems, we have identified that, in practice, this is difficult
for the collective systems to implement, as CALM does not usually appoint representatives to the supervisory
body, or does not have a position on this matter, due to a lack of available personnel.

2.3.2.    Regulation on packaging and packaging waste

The Regulation on packaging and packaging waste11 partially transposes Directive 94/62/EC on packaging
and packaging waste into national law. Similar to the battery sector, at the EU level we are in a legislative
transition period, from the legal regime established by the old Packaging Directive to the new legal regime
established by Regulation (EU) 2025/40 on packaging and packaging waste. It entered into force on February
11, 2025, and applies as of August 12, 2026. Directive 94/62/EC is repealed as of the date of entry into force
of Regulation (EU) 2025/40, except for certain provisions that remain in force until the dates expressly specified
in the Regulation for each provision, but no later than January 1, 2030.
Among the provisions that remain in force are, for example, the rules on the essential requirements applicable
to packaging placed on the market (which must remain in force until new implementing acts are adopted); the
rules on recycling targets for packaging waste, namely the overall recycling target and the minimum targets by
material, such as glass, paper/cardboard, metals, plastic, and wood (continue to apply until December 31,
2028); the rules for calculating the achievement of targets; the obligations to report data to the Commission;
the obligation to submit a quality control report; and the Commission’s obligation to review and publish the
results of the data assessment.
Therefore, during the legislative analysis regarding compliance with the EU legal framework, we will primarily
refer to the need for alignment with the rules of the new EU legal framework, even though the current provisions
are aligned with Directive 94/62/EC, with the aim of highlighting priority legislative adjustments.
Scope of the EPR regime for packaging
The scope of the EPR Regulation on packaging covers “all packaging placed on the market, regardless of the
material from which it is made and the manner of use, whether originating from industry, offices, shops,
services, private households, or any other activities, as well as all packaging waste, regardless of how it is
generated.”
The main legal concepts and terms used in the EPR system for packaging are defined, including definitions
for the following types of packaging – packaging, primary packaging, secondary packaging, tertiary packaging,
reusable packaging, SD reusable packaging, as well as other types of packaging, such as retail packaging,
single-use packaging, composite packaging, etc. The definitions in the EPR Regulation on packaging are
supplemented by or refer to Law No. 209/2016 on waste. Furthermore, operations related to the management
of packaging and packaging waste, such as reuse, recycling, and refilling, are defined.
We note, regarding the definition of “oxo-biodegradable bags” (Point 5, subsection 15), that in the European
Union these are prohibited under Article 5 of the SUP Directive12 , which bans all products made from oxo-
degradable plastics.
The EPR Regulation on packaging covers general aspects of the packaging regime, such as prevention
obligations, both for the state—which develops and promotes waste prevention programs—and for producers,
who will implement measures to prevent the generation of packaging waste, measures to reduce the
consumption of certain types of materials, packaging, and products, and other measures to reduce the
consumption of thin plastic carrier bags.
These provisions supplement the obligations of producers and authorized operators for packaging waste
management to implement the priorities in packaging and packaging waste management set forth in Law No.
209/2016, Art. 54, namely: to implement measures to prevent waste generation, reuse, recycle into usable
products or secondary raw materials suitable for the manufacture of such products, recover energy, and
dispose of waste safely.
Law No. 209/2016 provides for an exemption from EPR obligations regarding packaging and introduces a
simplified regime for producers who place small quantities of packaging on the market, namely no more than
100 kg/year (Art. 54(10)). These producers are exempt from EPR obligations, from the obligation to pay the
environmental pollution fee related to packaging waste, as well as from EPR record-keeping, reporting, and
registration obligations. However, the exemption does not apply to reusable packaging and single-use
packaging subject to the deposit system. When calculating the 100 kg/year threshold, the weight of pallets
used exclusively for transport is not included, provided they are managed in a closed-loop system and do not
reach the end consumer. Producers benefiting from this exemption must, however, submit a sworn statement

How fulfilment of EPR obligations for packaging is regulated
Under Moldovan law, manufacturers have limited options to fulfil their EPR obligations through an individual
system. This is in line with EU legislation, which allows Member States to adopt measures to make it mandatory
to entrust the task of fulfilling producers’ EPR obligations to an EPR organization (Regulation (EU) 2025/40,
Art. 46(1)).
According to Law No. 209/2006 on waste, “the management of packaging and packaging waste (...) shall be
carried out as follows:
        a) individually—for packaging used for one’s own purposes, by organizing systems for the take-back
        or collection and recovery thereof, (...), or by handing it over to an authorized operator;
        b) collectively—for packaging placed on the market, by organizing systems for its take-back or
        collection and recovery, (...);
        c) through participation in the deposit system (...).” (Art. 54, para. (3)).
The EPR Regulation on packaging details, through various provisions, how EPR obligations are to be fulfilled,
namely the possibility for producers to choose between the individual and collective systems, and these
provisions, taken together, lead to the conclusion that the producer’s option for the individual system is limited
to “packaging used for own purposes,” within a closed system. This option cannot be considered permissible
“for products intended for users other than private households,” which would imply a broader scope of
application for the individual system.
Points 14 and 24 regulate how producers may fulfil their EPR obligations, with membership in a collective
system being mandatory “for products marketed for consumption in households” (Point 14, subsection 2).
Producers may choose to fulfil their EPR obligations individually (individual system) only for products intended
for users other than households, including products used by the producer for its own purposes (Point 14,
subsection 1), Point 24, subsection 2).
In addition, Points 101and 102 establish the obligation for producers to fulfil their EPR obligations through a
collective system based on the type and use of the packaging, a factor that further restricts the applicability of
the individual system:
    -   The collective system is mandatory for primary packaging (when products are intended for end
        consumers), secondary packaging intended to group a certain number of products to facilitate handling
        and transport, as well as packaging used exclusively for logistics and distribution that is not reusable;
    -   Primary packaging used exclusively at the producer’s own points of sale (cafes, catering), secondary
        packaging used for handling or storage within the manufacturing company (transport boxes for internal
        delivery), reusable tertiary packaging or durable packaging used in logistics that is managed in a
        closed-loop system, without reaching the end consumer or being placed on the market.
The entities subject to the obligations arising from responsibility for packaging that has become waste,
and the scope of responsibility for each type of entity concerned, as an embodiment of the principle of extended
producer responsibility, are set forth in Point 13:
    -   producers who place packaged products on the market—for packaging waste generated by primary,
        secondary, and tertiary packaging used to package the products they place on the market, with the
        exception of retail packaging;
    -   producers who over-package packaged products for resale or redistribution on the market—for the
        packaging used for over-packaging;
    -   manufacturers who manufacture/import retail packaging, including plastic shopping bags, for placing
        on the market – for such packaging;
    -   manufacturers who rent out packaging, in any form, on a commercial basis—for such packaging.
        Reusable packaging (crates, pallets, bottles, plastic distribution packaging (buckets, boxes)) is subject
        to reporting upon first placing on the market.

year for which the payment obligation must be determined, even if the EPR Regulation imposes a shorter
deadline.
ii) Article 29 of Law No. 209/2016 on Waste – this provision establishes the obligation of an individual or
collective system that has not met the targets corresponding to its EPR obligations to bear the operational
costs of waste management by paying for the difference between the quantity corresponding to the target and
the quantity actually managed (para. (4)a).
Taken together, the two provisions referred to in Point 22 of the EPR Regulation on packaging effectively lead
to a doubling of the penalty/payment for the same quantities of waste that should be managed according to
the target, and which are not actually managed due to the collective or individual system’s failure to meet the
target. In the case of producers who fulfil their EPR obligations through a collective system, the packaging fee
is borne by the producers, and the operational management cost for the quantity difference until the target is
met is borne by the collective system. In the case of the individual system, it is clear that the producer is liable
for both payments. Beyond the legal dispute—whether or not we have a situation of double taxation—in fact,
the application of the two types of payments for the same uncollected quantity leads to a disproportionate
economic burden for the respective producers, and is discriminatory between different EPR streams, which is
not justified by the greater environmental impact or risk of the packaging.
As an additional penalty, or as a preventive measure to ensure compliance with EPR obligations, the
Packaging Regulation establishes the obligation for a producer who fails to meet their recovery targets through
an individual system to join a collective system starting in the year following the one in which they failed to
meet these targets.

Monitoring of EPR activities
Regarding the applicable verification procedures, the Agency verifies the reports submitted by the individual

One observation regarding these requirements for verifying the EPR reports of collective systems relates to
the requirement concerning “the application of the same fees for assuming responsibility for packaging waste
management to all producers/authorized representatives (...)” (Para. 13 of Annex 9), which may represent a
barrier (or an argument for non-implementation) to modulated EPR fees. We note that Law No. 209/2016

the recyclability of packaging, a framework for the modulation of EPR financial contributions “based on the
performance classes of packaging in terms of recyclability.” In addition, Art. 7(7) allows for further modulation
of EPR contributions based on the recycled content used in packaging. Starting in 2030, EU-wide obligations
regarding the minimum recycled content in plastic packaging will apply, ranging from 10% to 30% depending
on the type of packaging.

3. Institutional Framework
Although the legal framework governing packaging waste management and extended producer responsibility
is relatively well-developed, its implementation continues to be limited by both persistent legislative and
procedural gaps and broader structural deficiencies affecting the waste management sector. In practice,
implementation is hampered by insufficient infrastructure for separate collection, limited sorting and recycling
capacities, weak enforcement and control mechanisms, fragmented reporting systems, and the continued
prevalence of a linear waste management model, based largely on the collection and disposal of mixed waste.
At the same time, institutional capacities, both at the national and local levels, remain limited, affecting the
monitoring, verification, and effective enforcement of EPR obligations regarding packaging waste.

3.1. Public institutions
The implementation of the Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) system for packaging waste in the
Republic of Moldova is based on a multi-level institutional framework involving central and local public
authorities, organizations implementing extended producer responsibility, producers and importers, waste
collection and recycling operators, municipal sanitation service providers, retailers, consumers, and
development partners.
Given that a significant portion of packaging waste is generated within the municipal waste stream, the effective
implementation of the EPR system for packaging depends on coordinated interaction between public
authorities, collective EPR systems, and waste management operators throughout the entire waste
management chain. This includes activities related to the placing of packaging on the market, the financing of
EPR obligations, separate collection, sorting, transport, recycling, reporting, monitoring, and compliance
control.
The institutional framework continues to evolve, including in the context of the future implementation of the
national deposit system for beverage packaging (SDA), which is expected to have a significant impact on
collection methods, the allocation of operational responsibilities, and the financing mechanisms applicable to
packaging waste management in the Republic of Moldova.
3.1.1.   National public institutions and authorities

Ministry of Environment
The Ministry of the Environment is the primary public authority responsible for developing policies and
legislation in the field of waste management in the Republic of Moldova, including the regulatory framework
for EPR for packaging and packaging waste. The Ministry develops and promotes regulations that define
producers’ obligations, set collection and recycling targets, and establish the institutional framework within
which individual and collective EPR systems operate. The Ministry coordinates the Republic of Moldova’s
commitments related to the EU accession process under the environment chapter, oversees the Environmental
Agency, and collaborates with the Ministry of Finance and the State Tax Service regarding EPR-related fiscal
instruments.
A specific and long-standing responsibility of the Ministry in the packaging sector is the development and
oversight of the deposit system for beverage containers, which is currently at an advanced stage of
implementation. The Ministry is the competent authority for designating the deposit system administrator and
for establishing the regulatory framework within which the deposit system operates.
A persistent challenge has been the high frequency of legislative changes, often introduced without sufficient
consultation with the business community, which has created legal uncertainty and compliance difficulties.
Strengthening the coherence of the regulatory drafting process and ensuring systematic consultation prior to
the adoption of legislative changes is an important institutional priority, alongside the public policy agenda
itself.
The Environment Agency
The Environmental Agency is the primary regulatory and administrative authority responsible for the day-to-
day operation of the EPR system for packaging and packaging waste. Its mandate covers the entire
administrative cycle, from authorizing collective and individual EPR systems to verifying annual performance
reports and calculating national collection and recycling rates.
Regarding the registration of producers, the Agency manages the national register of producers and importers
of packaging and packaged products falling under the scope of the packaging EPR framework. It reviews and
processes registration applications, monitors compliance, and is responsible—in cooperation with the State

Tax Service and the Customs Service—for identifying operators who have placed packaged products on the
market without fulfilling their registration obligations. Given the very large number of companies potentially
subject to obligations within the packaging stream—which includes, in practice, nearly all importers and
manufacturers of consumer goods—this function is considerably more demanding than in the case of WEEE
or batteries, and non-compliance through non-participation in the system is, consequently, more difficult to
identify and address.
The Agency is the authority responsible for authorizing collective and individual EPR systems for packaging:
it reviews applications, issues authorizations, and monitors ongoing compliance with operational, financial, and
reporting requirements. The Agency also manages the SIA MD platform, through which producers, collective
systems, and operators annually report the quantities placed on the market, collected, and recycled, and this

A specific function relevant to the packaging sector is the Agency’s role in relation to the deposit system for

SDA obligations and is responsible for integrating the data generated by this system into the broader EPR
reporting framework for packaging.
As with other streams subject to the EPR, the Agency’s ability to exercise rigorous oversight is limited by
insufficient human and technical resources. Strengthening this capacity—as well as the Agency’s ability to
provide consistent and authoritative guidance to the broad and diverse community of companies subject to the
specific legal regime in the packaging sector—is essential for improving compliance and reducing legal
uncertainty.
Environmental Protection Inspectorate
The Environmental Protection Inspectorate is responsible for monitoring compliance with environmental and
waste management legislation. It conducts inspections of producers, collective systems, operators, and local
service providers and imposes administrative sanctions in cases of non-compliance, illegal waste disposal,
unauthorized activities, or failure to fulfil EPR obligations.
State Tax Service
The State Tax Service supports the implementation of the EPR framework by administering the environmental
pollution tax. It verifies the declarations submitted by producers and importers and provides an important
mechanism for tax compliance by linking tax exemptions to proof of fulfilment of producer responsibility
obligations through authorized systems.
Customs Service
Customs authorities contribute to the implementation of the EPR by monitoring imported products placed on
the market and by assisting in the identification of undeclared or non-compliant imports. Coordination among
customs, tax, and environmental authorities is important for reducing non-participation in the system and for
improving the accuracy of producer registration.

3.2. Producers and producer organizations
Producers, importers, and traders
Producers, importers, distributors, retailers, and distance sellers who place packaged products on the

include registering with the competent authorities, reporting the quantities of packaging placed on the market,
financing the collection and treatment of packaging waste, ensuring compliance with recovery and recycling
targets, and fulfilling consumer information and awareness obligations.
Obligated producers may fulfil their EPR obligations either individually or through collective compliance

the Republic of Moldova.
Producers, business organizations, and sectoral associations play a key role in disseminating information
regarding the obligations arising from extended producer responsibility. They facilitate communication between
regulatory authorities and economic operators and actively participate in consultations on the development
and revision of the regulatory framework applicable to packaging waste management.

3.3. Stakeholders in the waste management and recycling sector
The operational model established by the EPR framework in the Republic of Moldova for regulated waste
streams is based on a restricted collection system, within which the collection activities of independent
operators are closely linked to treatment authorizations and/or collective EPR systems.
According to Law No. 209/2016 on waste, the collection of waste streams resulting from products subject to
the EPR regime may be carried out exclusively either by individual or collective systems implementing EPR
obligations, or by operators authorized to treat such waste and contracted by those systems (Art. 12, para.
(18)). Consequently, the legal framework does not permit independent operators holding only collection and/or
transport authorizations to carry out collection activities for waste streams regulated by the EPR scheme
independently of collective systems. Furthermore, under the law, they will not be able to renew the collection
permits under which they currently operate unless they also carry out or are authorized for treatment activities.
In practice, this has led to the development of a relatively closed operational model for waste streams subject
to the EPR system, in which collection activities are effectively contingent upon the existence of treatment
capacities and the corresponding authorizations. Existing authorized operators continue to carry out collection
activities based on valid authorizations and contractual arrangements concluded with collective systems.
According to information provided during the consultations conducted for this study, after the current
authorizations expire, it is anticipated that collection activities for waste streams subject to EPR will increasingly
be carried out directly by collective systems.
This approach is likely to further strengthen the operational role of collective systems within the waste
management chain, while reducing the role of independent collection operators that are not integrated into
treatment structures or structures for compliance with EPR obligations. At the same time, the transition to direct
collection by collective systems may require further clarification regarding authorization obligations, operational
responsibilities, oversight mechanisms, and the allocation of liability between EPR entities and contracted
waste management operators.
Authorized collection, treatment, and recycling operators – formal sector
Authorized operators for the collection, sorting, dismantling/disassembly, recycling, and treatment of waste
provide the technical and operational infrastructure necessary for the implementation of EPR schemes in the
Republic of Moldova. This includes the collection, transport, temporary storage, sorting, dismantling, recycling,
and recovery of waste streams subject to EPR obligations, as well as the documentation of recovered materials
and the reporting of treatment results to collective systems and competent authorities.
Their role is particularly important given the limited domestic recycling and treatment capacities available for
certain waste streams. For several categories of waste, including WEEE and waste batteries and
accumulators, collection and pre-treatment activities are carried out within the Republic of Moldova, while final
treatment and recycling are performed abroad, through export to authorized treatment facilities in EU member
states.
Public utility companies and municipal sanitation operators are also key players in the waste management
chain, particularly for packaging waste and other waste streams generated within the municipal waste stream.

The informal sector, which includes independent collectors, waste pickers, and recyclable material dealers,

3.4. Consumers and civil society
Consumers and institutional end-users
Households, businesses, public institutions, and other end users are key participants in the packaging waste
management chain and play an important role in the effective implementation of the EPR. The performance of
separate collection systems depends largely on the willingness and ability of waste generators to properly
separate packaging waste and direct it to formal collection channels.
Public participation is directly influenced by factors such as the accessibility and convenience of the collection
infrastructure, the frequency and quality of collection services, the level of public awareness regarding separate
collection obligations, and the degree of confidence in the efficiency of recycling systems. In practice, limited
source separation, insufficient awareness, and the continued prevalence of mixed municipal waste collection
continue to affect the quality and quantity of recyclable packaging waste reaching formal recovery and
recycling systems.
Civil society and stakeholders involved in information and awareness-raising activities
Civil society organizations, educational institutions, consumer associations, and media actors contribute to
raising environmental awareness and promoting responsible waste management practices, including the
separate collection and recycling of packaging waste. Their activities support public information campaigns,
behaviour change initiatives, and the dissemination of information regarding the environmental impact
associated with improper waste disposal.
Their role is particularly important in the context of the gradual development of EPR systems and separate
collection infrastructure in the Republic of Moldova, where public participation and trust in the system remain
essential for improving collection rates and increasing the efficiency of recycling systems.

3.5.1.   Governance and institutional coordination

Unclear division of responsibilities between EPR organizations and local public administration
authorities

The legal framework does not clearly define the operational and financial responsibilities of local public
administration authorities and collective systems regarding the establishment and operation of separate
collection and of packaging waste. Existing legislation provides only limited guidance on the content of
cooperation agreements between municipalities and organizations implementing EPR, and practical
implementation is frequently based on case-by-case negotiations. This situation has led to inconsistent
approaches among municipalities regarding financing mechanisms, infrastructure ownership, operational
responsibilities, and the provision of collection services.
Limited institutional capacity for implementation and enforcement
The competent authorities responsible for supervising and enforcing packaging waste legislation continue to
face limited institutional and administrative capacity. Challenges persist regarding monitoring compliance,
verifying reported data, identifying non-compliant obligated companies, and ensuring the effective enforcement
of EPR obligations. Institutional fragmentation among environmental, tax, customs, and statistical authorities
further limits the effectiveness of oversight and coordination mechanisms.
Regulatory and control gaps regarding the Transnistria region
Although legislation establishes specific provisions regarding products placed on the market from territory not
controlled by the constitutional authorities of the Republic of Moldova, EPR obligations are not effectively
implemented or enforced in practice in this area. This situation creates unequal market conditions, control
gaps, and potential risks of underreporting.
Operational difficulties related to the mandatory participation of external institutional representatives
Stakeholders have reported practical difficulties regarding the participation of certain mandatory institutional
representatives within the oversight bodies of collective systems. In particular, the limited responsiveness of
some external organizations (such as CALM) may affect the effective functioning of these bodies, even though
collective systems remain formally responsible for the related obligations.
Incomplete integration of emerging EPR mechanisms
The regulatory and operational framework for the future beverage container deposit system (SDA) is in a
transitional phase. On May 20, 2026, the Government approved the decision designating the Public
Association “Eco-Ret Moldova” as the administrator of the national deposit system (SD). According to the
applicable regulatory framework, the system must become fully operational no later than January 1, 2027.
At the same time, the designation process and the system’s operational launch have faced delays and
institutional challenges. The designation of the SDA administrator was delayed by several months compared
to the initial implementation timeline, partly due to disagreements among competing industry stakeholders,
disputes regarding market representativeness and the fulfilment of eligibility criteria, as well as broader
discussions regarding the governance and operational control of the future system.
As a result, the recently appointed administrator is currently under significant time pressure to establish the
operational, financial, and logistical infrastructure necessary for the nationwide implementation of the deposit
system within the timeframe set by law. This includes developing the collection infrastructure, concluding
contractual arrangements with retailers and operators, establishing logistics and reporting systems, as well as
preparing the financial and governance mechanisms necessary for the system’s operation.
The interaction between the future deposit system and the existing EPR system for packaging remains only
partially clarified, particularly regarding the allocation of operational and financial responsibilities among the
SDA administrator, municipalities, waste management operators, and existing collective EPR systems for
packaging waste.
At the same time, recent developments at the EU level regarding the implementation and further strengthening
of the Single-Use Plastics Directive (Directive (EU) 2019/904), as well as the adoption of the new Regulation
(EU) on packaging and packaging waste, introduce additional EPR obligations for certain single-use plastic
products, including obligations regarding the collection of litter, public cleaning costs, awareness-raising
measures, and requirements for recycled content. These requirements have not yet been fully transposed or
integrated into the national legal framework of the Republic of Moldova, which may lead to future alignment
and implementation challenges.
Risk of duplicating costs for managing the same waste
The EPR Regulation on Packaging (Point 22) establishes the obligation for producers to bear the costs
associated with waste placed on the market if annual recycling targets are not met. The regulation refers to
two types of payments/fees, provided for in two legislative acts: the packaging fee rate in accordance with Art.
11 of Law No. 1540/1998, which, in practice, translates into the payment of a packaging fee for a quantity of
                                                                                                               42
Study on Packaging Waste Management in the Republic of Moldova




products/packaging greater than the difference between the target and the quantity recovered, according to
the calculation method set forth in the law. The second payment results from the obligation of the individual or
collective system that has not met its targets corresponding to EPR obligations to bear the operational costs
of waste management, by paying for the difference between the quantity corresponding to the target and the
quantity actually managed (Art. 29 of Law No. 209/2016 on waste, para. (4)(a)). In addition to these payments,
there are penalties that the IPM may impose for non-compliance with legal obligations regarding recovery
targets.
Taken together, the two rules result in a doubling of the penalty/payment for the same quantities of waste that
should be managed according to the target, and which are not actually managed due to the collective or
individual system failing to meet the target. For producers who fulfil their EPR obligations through a collective
system, the packaging fee is borne by the producers, and the operational management cost for the quantity
difference until the target is met is borne by the collective system. In the case of the individual system, the
producer is liable for both payments. The application of the two types of payments for the same unrecycled
quantity results in a disproportionate economic burden on those producers and constitutes discrimination
between different EPR streams, which is not justified by the greater environmental impact or risk of the
packaging.
3.5.2.   Legal status, authorization, and market structure of collective EPR systems

Inconsistencies in the legal requirements applicable to organizations implementing EPR
The legal framework contains a number of inconsistencies regarding the organizational and operational
requirements applicable to collective systems. These include issues related to the mandatory legal status of
the collective system as a non-profit organization, ownership structures, minimum market share requirements,
and operational capacity criteria. The current framework does not always ensure a clear distinction between
compliance functions, operational waste management activities, and commercial activities carried out within
the waste management chain.
Unequal conditions between collective and individual EPR compliance systems
Collective systems are subject to more extensive operational and administrative requirements than individual
compliance systems, which can create unequal conditions for compliance and affect competitive neutrality
within the EPR system. This imbalance warrants analysis in any review of the regulatory framework.
The fragmented market for EPR systems and the absence of a “clearing house” mechanism
The legal framework does not establish minimum thresholds for operational efficiency or market share for
collective systems, leading to a fragmented landscape that is disproportionate to the size of the Moldovan
market. A large number of small-scale EPR collective systems increases the administrative burden, reduces
the financial resources available for investment in collection infrastructure, and increases the complexity of
oversight activities. This fragmentation is exacerbated by the absence of a “clearing house” mechanism to
perform central coordination and clearing functions among collective systems. Currently, there is no entity
responsible for coordination among collective systems, for verifying market share declarations, for allocating
collection obligations, or for cross-checking performance reported at the system level. In the absence of this
coordination and balancing mechanism—whether its functions would be performed by a dedicated public or
private body or by the Environmental Agency with enhanced powers and capabilities —it is not possible to
reliably verify whether national collection targets are actually being met, to identify producers evading EPR
obligations, or to ensure a fair allocation of obligations among collective systems. Market consolidation,
supported by clearer minimum criteria, and the establishment of a “clearing house” mechanism are closely
linked priority actions. We note here that the new EU Packaging Regulation requires Member States to
establish such a mechanism, defined as “an independent third party tasked with overseeing that organizations
implementing extended producer responsibility fulfil their EPR obligations in a coordinated manner, or [Member
States] entrust such a supervisory task to the competent authority” (Art. 46, para. (2)).
Restrictions on changing membership in collective systems
Law No. 209/2016 allows producers to join or withdraw from a collective system only at the end of the calendar
year (Art. 12, para. (8²)). In practice, this provision may limit producers’ ability to migrate to another EPR
collective system under more favourable conditions and may lock them into underperforming systems,
particularly in situations where failure to meet targets becomes evident only after the annual reporting
deadlines.
3.5.3.   Verification, audit, and data integrity

These issues represent some of the most significant structural vulnerabilities affecting the current EPR
framework for packaging. Currently, there is no fully coherent and independently verified control chain that

links the quantities of packaging placed on the market with the quantities separately collected, sorted, recycled,
and ultimately reported as contributions toward achieving recovery and recycling targets.
Each stage of this chain—including manufacturers, importers, collective systems, collection and sorting
operators, recyclers, exporters, and local government authorities—operates with limited requirements for
independent verification and auditing. As a result, the reliability and consistency of reported data remain difficult
to validate independently, and discrepancies between quantities placed on the market, waste collected, and
materials recycled cannot always be effectively identified or reconciled.

Limited granularity of reporting and lack of methodological clarity

The current reporting framework for packaging waste remains focused on the main categories of packaging
materials and does not sufficiently differentiate between reusable and single-use packaging, between
household and commercial packaging, or between various packaging categories and product types. This
creates limitations for the future implementation of eco-modulation mechanisms for producers’ financial
contributions and more advanced fee structures within the EPR system. Furthermore, the legislation does not
provide sufficiently detailed methodologies for determining, documenting, and reporting the quantities of
packaging placed on the market, including the relationship between packaging declarations, accounting
records, and tax reporting systems. These issues must also be assessed in the context of reporting obligations
to the EU, with a view to accession, such as the new reporting requirements in the EU Packaging Regulation.
Audit of producers’ declarations submitted to collective systems
Producers report the quantities of packaging they place on the market to the collective system to which they
have transferred their EPR obligations, and these declarations form the basis for calculating EPR financial
contributions. Currently, there is no requirement for these declarations to be independently audited or cross-
checked against other company declarations or data, such as sales records or customs data. This creates a
significant risk of underreporting—whether deliberate or due to inconsistent methodologies—and means that
collective systems may collect insufficient financial contributions relative to their actual obligations (for
example, even if they meet their targets, collective systems for packaging must ensure the continuity of
collection throughout the year). Clear guidance is needed on how producers should document and report the

Study on Packaging Waste Management in the Republic of Moldova




quantities placed on the market, how these should be reconciled with accounting and tax records, and how
collective systems should verify the credibility of the declarations received from members. A legal requirement
for external audits of producers’ declarations should be considered—at least for larger producers.
Audit of the annual reports of collective systems submitted to the Environmental Agency
The annual reports submitted by collective systems to the Environmental Agency are not subject to an
independent audit prior to submission. This means that the primary performance data on which authorities rely
to assess the achievement of EPR targets has not been independently verified. The mandatory external audit
of collective system operations, conducted once every three years as required by Law No. 209/2016, is
considered insufficient to ensure compliance. The reports of collective systems should be subject to a
mandatory independent operational audit each year—covering both the accuracy of data on collection and
recycling and the fulfilment of contractual obligations—before being submitted to the competent authorities.
The methodology, scope, and qualifications required for such audits should be defined in legislation to ensure
consistency across systems. Furthermore, the annual reports of collective systems are not currently accessible
to the public, which limits accountability to municipalities and civil society. The publication of audited annual
reports should be a standard transparency requirement.
Verification of data from waste management operators and local public authorities
In the case of packaging waste, local public authorities and waste management operators are the primary
channel through which packaging in municipal waste is collected from the public. Law No. 178/2025 on public
waste management services establishes the obligation of separate collection by waste stream, and Law No.
209/2016 stipulates that collective EPR systems for packaging are required to compensate for the costs
associated with the collection of packaging waste through public waste management services. Consequently,
data on the quantities of packaging collected separately through municipal services form the basis for both the
calculation of national recycling targets and the financial compensation mechanism between collective
systems and local public authorities.
However, the regulatory framework does not clearly establish the method for recording, reporting, and verifying
the quantities of packaging waste collected through sanitation services and subsequently delivered to sorting
and recycling facilities. There are no standardized methodologies for tracking these quantities throughout the
entire chain—from separate household collection, through sorting stations, to recycling facilities.
This gap has direct consequences for the reliability of national data on packaging recycling: the same quantities
may be reported by multiple actors in the chain, data provided by waste management operators may not be
reconciled with that reported by sorting facilities, and the Environmental Agency lacks the necessary tools to
verify the consistency of these data flows. In the absence of clear reporting and verification standards
applicable to all actors in the chain—waste management operators, sorting facilities, collective systems, and
local public authorities—the calculation of national packaging recycling targets remains vulnerable to
inconsistencies and over-reporting.
Insufficient verification and audit mechanisms
The quantities of packaging placed on the market, as reported by obligated companies, are generally not
subject to mandatory independent auditing or systematic verification. Monitoring mechanisms designed to
identify unregistered producers and reduce non-participation in the system remain limited in practice. The
absence of periodic cross-checks between environmental reports, customs data, and tax records reduces the
authorities’ ability to verify the accuracy and completeness of the reported data.
The Role of Tax Authorities in verifying EPR obligations
The State Tax Service administers the environmental pollution tax provided for by Law No. 1540/1998, from
which producers are exempt if they can demonstrate compliance with EPR obligations (achieving recovery
targets). This mechanism creates a natural point for cross-checking: the State Tax Service already receives
tax returns from producers and importers and is in a suitable position to verify whether companies requesting
exemptions based on the fulfilment of EPR obligations are actually registered in an authorized collective
system and have fulfilled their obligations. Formalizing the exchange of information between the Environmental
Agency and the State Tax Service—including by sharing data on producer registration, declarations regarding
quantities placed on the market, and EPR compliance status—would strengthen the identification of operators
not participating in the system and reduce opportunities to evade both EPR obligations and the environmental
pollution tax.
Limited transparency in reporting by collective systems
The annual reports submitted by collective systems are not publicly available, which limits transparency and
public oversight of EPR implementation. Furthermore, the annual reports focus primarily on meeting recycling
                                                                                                              45
Study on Packaging Waste Management in the Republic of Moldova




targets and provide only limited information on operational performance, infrastructure investments, the
development of separate collection systems, awareness campaigns, and service quality indicators.
Furthermore, annual reports are not subject to a mandatory independent audit prior to submission to the
competent authorities.
Instability of the digital reporting platform
Stakeholders have reported recurring instability and limited functionality of the national electronic reporting
platform, SIA MD, which creates practical difficulties in the reporting process, data transmission, and the
fulfilment of administrative compliance procedures. These technical deficiencies affect the reliability of
reporting, delay the transmission of information, and reduce the effectiveness of monitoring and verification
activities carried out by the competent authorities.
3.5.4.   Operational gaps

Insufficient technical and operational requirements
The current legal framework does not establish sufficiently detailed technical requirements, operational criteria,
or performance indicators applicable to separate collection systems for packaging waste. This limits the
consistency of implementation and leads to differences in service quality, collection frequency, and
infrastructure availability among different municipalities and operators.
Structural challenges affecting implementation at the local level
The implementation of separate collection systems is further hampered by structural factors such as the small
size of municipalities, low population density, and the limited financial capacities of local public administration
authorities and private operators. Many companies active in the sector are small businesses with limited
investment capacity, while private-sector involvement in separate collection and sorting services remains
relatively low.
Low public participation and awareness
Public awareness of separate collection obligations and the environmental risks associated with the improper
disposal of packaging waste remains limited. Household participation in separate collection systems remains
relatively low, affecting both the quantity and quality of recyclable packaging waste entering formal recycling
systems.
The lack of a standard cooperation agreement between organizations implementing EPR and local
public administration authorities, as required by law
The legislation provides for the approval of a standard model contract to regulate cooperation between local
public administration authorities and collective systems; however, the model has not yet been adopted,
creating legal and procedural uncertainty for the implementation of cooperation agreements at the local level.
Frequent legislative changes and limited stakeholder consultation
Stakeholders have repeatedly highlighted the high frequency of changes to waste management legislation and
implementing regulations, often introduced without sufficient consultation with the affected sectors. This
situation creates legal uncertainty, increases compliance costs, complicates long-term planning, and reduces
predictability for producers, collective systems, and waste management operators.
Addressing these institutional and procedural gaps will require stronger institutional coordination, harmonized
reporting and verification mechanisms, a clearer allocation of responsibilities, and the strengthening of
administrative capacity for monitoring and control. In the absence of these measures, the EPR system for
packaging risks remaining focused primarily on formal compliance rather than on actual environmental
performance and the real achievement of recycling targets.
Lack of a formal mechanism for involving the informal sector
Informal packaging collectors operate in a manner similar to that of formal recycling—they collect recyclable
material, sort it, and sell it to processing facilities. This characteristic makes them easier to integrate into the
formal system, provided there are adequate channels and incentives. The current framework does not provide
for specific mechanisms through which informal collectors of recyclable materials can be registered, report the
quantities collected, and receive financial compensation from collective EPR systems for materials delivered
to authorized facilities. The creation of such mechanisms—including framework agreements between
collective systems and small-scale collectors, pay-per-ton schemes, and minimum documentation
requirements—could gradually formalize this sector, increase officially reported volumes, and help meet
packaging recycling targets at a low marginal cost. Furthermore, formalizing informal packaging collectors
                                                                                                                 46
Study on Packaging Waste Management in the Republic of Moldova




would expand the collection system’s geographic coverage to rural and peri-urban areas where formal
infrastructure is absent or insufficient.
In conclusion, among the issues outlined above, the most significant structural shortcoming remains the
absence of a coherent chain of verification and audit: from the quantities of products entering the market, to
manufacturers’ declarations to collective systems, to the reporting of collective and individual EPR systems to
the Environmental Agency, up to the contributions of municipalities and complementary operators. Without
independent audit requirements at each of these stages, the reported performance data cannot be trusted, the
evasion of responsibility cannot be detected, and the system cannot credibly demonstrate whether it is meeting
its objectives. In addition, the fragmented market for collective systems, the absence of a “clearing house”
function or mechanism, unclear municipal roles, and limited enforcement capacity—all exacerbate the gap
between the legal framework, on paper, and its performance in practice. Addressing these gaps requires
targeted legislative changes, institutional cooperation—particularly between the Environmental Agency, the
Customs Service, and the State Tax Service—clearer guidance documents, and sustained investment in the
administrative capacity of the competent authorities.

4. Management of packaging waste
4.1. Municipal waste management – the broader context of EPR
implementation
The waste management sector in the Republic of Moldova remains one of the least developed in the region.
Nationally, over 1.1 million tons of household waste are generated annually, while the recycling rate remains
below 10%. The system has historically operated on the basis of an approach prioritizing disposal: currently,
approximately 1,139 landfills are in operation, covering a total area of 1,224 hectares. These landfills are
managed by local public administration authorities and generally do not meet environmental standards. In
addition, over 2,000 illegal landfills remain active across the country, and a significant proportion of collected
waste continues to be disposed of in non-compliant landfills.
In Chișinău, approximately 90% of the waste collected by sanitation services is disposed of in landfills, while
separate collection is only partially organized in the capital and in some district centres. Many recyclable
materials—paper, glass, metals, and plastics—are landfilled alongside non-recyclable waste, thereby losing
much of their potential value.
4.1.1.   Municipal waste management and regionalization

Moldova has structured its waste management reform around a system consisting of eight waste management
zones (WMZs), achieving significant, albeit uneven, progress. Region 5 (Ungheni, Nisporeni, and Călărași) is
the most advanced, with a tender already launched for the design and construction of the necessary
infrastructure. Similar procedures are underway for Region 8 (Briceni, Edineț, Dondușeni, and Ocnița). In April
2025, feasibility studies were launched for Region 4 (Chișinău, Strășeni, Ialoveni, Hâncești, Criuleni, Anenii
Noi, and Orhei) and Region 7 (Bălți and the northern districts). In Region 1 (Cahul, Cantemir, Taraclia, Ceadâr-
Lunga, Vulcănești, and the Gagauzia Autonomous Territorial Unit), the regional waste management operator
is currently being established, while in Region 2 (Leova, Cimișlia, Basarabeasca, Căușeni, and Ștefan-Vodă),
the feasibility study is still being developed.
The regionalization process aims to create a consolidated regional solid waste management system that
covers not only urban centres but also rural areas, and is expected to serve as a model for a sustainable
approach at the national level. The model provides for each household to receive two bins—one for recyclable
waste and one for non-recyclable waste—with the waste being transported to transfer stations where
recyclable materials are sorted and processed, and organic waste is turned into compost. Delays in
implementation led the Ministry of the Environment to declare the solid waste project a public utility of national
interest, which allows for simplified permitting procedures, the ability to carry out work in parallel with
expropriation proceedings, and faster access to the necessary land.
4.1.2.   Waste collection services

The coverage of waste collection services in the Republic of Moldova remains highly uneven, with a marked
disparity between urban and rural areas. In urban areas, the coverage rate for organized solid waste
management services is reported to be approximately 70–80%, while in rural areas coverage remains very
low—in many areas below 10%, and in some cases nearly non-existent. A significant portion of the rural
population continues to resort to unregulated waste disposal practices, including burning and dumping in
unauthorized locations.
Where organized collection services exist, they are provided through two main methods: communal collection
points equipped with shared containers—typically with a capacity of 0.7 m³ or 1.1 m³—and door-to-door
collection using individual household bins of 120 L or 240 L. Collection points are the dominant model in most
areas, while door-to-door collection is more common in larger urban centres. The existing fleet of containers
is outdated, with a significant proportion of containers and bins having been in use for more than five years
and requiring replacement.
The vehicle fleet used for waste collection is also outdated. Collection is predominantly carried out using
compactor trucks in urban areas and tractors in rural areas, most of which are old and technically unsuitable
for the requirements of a modern integrated waste management system. This situation limits both the
efficiency, and the coverage of collection services, and reduces the capacity for the large-scale introduction of
separate collection of recyclable fractions, including packaging waste.

4.1.3.   Source separation and selective collection of waste

Separate collection of packaging waste in the Republic of Moldova faces the same structural constraints as
the broader municipal waste management system — and, in some respects, is even more severely affected,
given that packaging is dispersed throughout the entire waste stream and can only be captured through a
collection system that consistently and extensively reaches citizens. Such a system does not yet exist in the
Republic of Moldova on a significant scale outside the municipality of Chișinău and a small number of district
centres.
The baseline situation is that only 75% of urban localities and 15% of rural localities have access to any
organized waste collection service, and where collection does exist, it is almost entirely mixed. Separately
collected waste accounts for approximately 7% of the total waste collected nationwide—a figure that includes
all separately collected fractions and illustrates the extent of the gap from the recycling targets aligned with EU
requirements that the Republic of Moldova has committed to achieving. Separate collection of packaging
fractions—paper and cardboard, plastic, metal, and glass—is partially implemented in some urban areas
through color-coded container networks, but these are absent in the vast majority of the country. Over 100
municipalities have received vehicles and containers through Environmental Fund programs in the past year,
which represents progress, but the vast majority of localities remain completely unequipped.
The financial structure of the municipal waste management sector makes progress structurally difficult. The
fees charged for waste collection in most municipalities in the Republic of Moldova are too low to cover the
actual cost of providing the service. Consequently, local authorities lack the necessary resources to invest
independently in separate collection infrastructure, and the sector as a whole faces financial constraints that
limit the pace of improvements, regardless of the existing regulatory framework.
The development of sorting, transfer, and treatment infrastructure through the regional waste management
zones program will, over time, create a more solid foundation for packaging waste management. As regional
transfer stations and sorting facilities become operational in the eight waste management zones, the physical
infrastructure needed to receive and process separately collected packaging fractions will improve. However,
this infrastructure alone will not solve the problem: it must be complemented by functional source-separated
collection, supported by adequate fees and EPR funding, as well as a level of consumer awareness that leads
to effective waste separation behaviour. All three of these conditions are currently absent or only partially met.

consumption. Regarding the quantities of packaging waste collected and recycled, the Environmental

The quantitative analysis of packaging waste starts with the quantities of waste generated. Data on generated
waste are available from the following main sources:
    -    The SIA MD reporting system;
    -    The Environmental Strategy for 2024–2030, which includes projections of packaging waste quantities;
    -    The USAID waste characterization study, conducted in certain regions of the Republic of Moldova in

The SIA MD reporting system provides a relatively accurate picture of the waste management system,

4.3.        Collection and recycling of packaging waste
The slow progress in achieving the targets can be explained by the fact that the collection and recycling value
chain in the Republic of Moldova is underdeveloped and largely concentrated in Chișinău.
Law No. 209/2016 on waste, specifically Art. 12, paras. (17)-(18) regarding the collection of packaging waste
subject to EPR obligations, stipulates that:
    -    the collection of packaging waste is prohibited for any entity, except for individual or collective EPR
         systems and authorized treatment, recycling, or recovery operators contracted by them—which means
         that waste collection companies and operators in the recycling sector may carry out collection activities
         only on the basis of a contract with an authorized EPR system;
    -    producers who fulfil their obligations collectively are required to collaborate, as a priority and on a
         contractual basis, with local public authorities (LPAs) to develop complementary systems for the
         separate collection of packaging waste.
In the context of packaging waste, these provisions have practical significance. Packaging constitutes a high-
volume waste stream generated by a wide range of producers, importers, and distributors and collected
through various channels—including street collection points, door-to-door collection, voluntary drop-off points,
and, where available, deposit-refund systems. The current model tends to concentrate operational
responsibility within collective EPR systems and restricts independent operators’ direct access to collection
activities outside the contractual framework of EPR. This approach may limit operational flexibility in the context
of a waste stream as diverse and geographically dispersed as that of packaging, raising questions about the
system’s ability to ensure adequate territorial coverage—particularly in rural areas.
4.3.1.   Assessment of the packaging collection system

The primary requirement for the collection of recyclable materials, including packaging waste, is source
separation by the waste generator—households, commercial premises, and institutions—followed by separate
collection, transport, and delivery to sorting or recycling facilities. However, the simplicity of this model is also
its main vulnerability: its effectiveness depends entirely on the behaviour of waste generators and on the
density and accessibility of the separate collection infrastructure, both of which remain underdeveloped in the
Republic of Moldova.
Currently, the Republic of Moldova does not have a nationally coordinated system for the separate collection
of packaging waste. Collection is organized at the level of waste management zones and districts by sanitation
operators contracted by local public administration authorities and, where collective EPR systems are active,
through collection points and additional containers installed by them. ECO SAVE, Moldcontrol, ReGREEN,
and other collective systems have developed networks of collection points for packaging fractions in the areas
where they operate, but coverage is uneven and heavily concentrated in Chișinău and larger urban centres.
The difference between urban and rural areas is the main structural feature of the current system. In urban
areas, particularly in Chișinău, there is infrastructure for the separate collection of packaging—including color-
coded containers for paper, plastic, and glass, installed by both sanitation operators and EPR collective
systems. In rural areas, separate collection of packaging waste is virtually non-existent: organized waste
collection services cover only a small portion of the rural population, and where they do exist, collection targets
almost exclusively mixed residual waste, without separating recyclable fractions.
The absence of a national operational plan defining minimum infrastructure requirements, geographic
coverage standards, and clear roles and responsibilities among the stakeholders involved—local public

4.3.2.   The role of the informal sector in packaging waste recycling

The informal sector plays a significantly different role in packaging waste management than it does in the
collection of WEEE and batteries, and its importance should be understood as such. Unlike end-of-life EEE
and batteries—where informal actors are often the primary collection channel, and their activities have serious
consequences for the environment and public health—informal activity in the packaging waste sector focuses
on recovering materials with positive market value, and its environmental impact is relatively low.
Informal collectors and itinerant collectors. The most visible informal actors in the packaging waste stream
are informal collectors and itinerant collectors, who recover recyclable packaging materials—primarily PET
bottles, cardboard, paper, aluminium cans, and glass—from mixed waste containers, landfills, and public
spaces. This activity is a familiar feature of the urban landscape in the Republic of Moldova and is driven solely
by the market value of the recovered materials. Informal collectors do not handle hazardous materials,
generate minimal disruption and environmental contamination through their collection activities, and, in
practice, help divert packaging materials from landfills—a contribution that, though unquantified and
unreported, is real.
Recyclable material dealers and aggregators. A network of small dealers and aggregators purchases
recyclable packaging fractions—cardboard, paper, plastic, and metals—from households, commercial spaces,
markets, and institutions, which they subsequently consolidate for sale to domestic recyclers or for export.
These operators occupy an intermediate position between informal collectors and the formal recycling market:
they are commercially motivated, often formally registered as commercial operators, and maintain working
relationships with authorized recycling companies, such as Candelux-Com for paper and Metalferos for metals.
Their activities partially overlap with those of formally authorized waste dealers, and the distinction between
the two categories is often unclear in practice.
Relationship with the formal recycling market. Informal packaging waste collectors typically feed the
collected materials directly into the formal recycling chain. A PET bottle collected by an informal collector and
sold to a dealer, who in turn sells it to an authorized recycler, follows a valid material flow, even if it remains
invisible to the EPR reporting systems. The main consequence is not environmental harm, but statistical
invisibility: quantities recovered through informal channels do not enter the SIA MD reporting system, are not

                                                                                                                                         54
Study on Packaging Waste Management in the Republic of Moldova




counted toward achieving EPR collection targets, and are not attributed to any producer responsibility
obligations. This means that official figures on packaging waste collection and recycling almost certainly
underestimate actual material recovery, while the EPR system receives no recognition—and generates no
funding—for the recycling activity represented by informally recovered materials.
Implications for the design of the EPR system. The appropriate public policy response to the informal
sector’s activity in the packaging waste sector is not its elimination, but its progressive integration. The informal
sector demonstrates that there is a market for recyclable packaging materials in the Republic of Moldova and
that citizens and small operators are willing to participate in material recovery when a financial incentive exists.
A well-designed EPR system should capitalize on this dynamic, not compete with it: by creating formal
registration channels for small collectors and aggregators, establishing transparent purchase prices for clean
and sorted packaging fractions, and linking revenues from EPR contributions to payments that make formal
participation financially attractive. Deposit systems, where introduced, have proven highly effective in
redirecting informal collection activities—including those of informal collectors—toward the formal system by
assigning a defined monetary value to each returned container. The experience of EU accession countries
that have introduced deposit systems suggests that a well-designed scheme can rapidly formalize a significant
portion of previously informal collection activities, simultaneously improving both data quality and material
recovery rates. This system will soon be launched in the Republic of Moldova as well and represents an
opportunity for the emergence of a more inclusive EPR system.
4.3.3.   Recycling and treatment

Regarding the treatment and recycling of packaging waste, producers and collective EPR systems are required
to enter into contracts with authorized treatment and recycling operators. Nationally, there are domestic
recycling capacities for certain packaging material streams, but these remain uneven, underdeveloped for
certain fractions, and insufficiently integrated into the formal EPR reporting system.
For paper and cardboard packaging, domestic recycling capacity is the most developed, with Candelux-Com
SRL processing over 70% of the paper waste collected nationwide. For glass packaging, capacity has recently
expanded following the opening of a new sorting and recycling facility in Chișinău in 2025, which doubled
processing capacity to 2,000 tons per month. For metal packaging, Metalferos SA and a growing number of
authorized private operators collect and export metal waste, as domestic processing capacity is limited. For
plastic packaging—the most problematic stream and the one with the highest volumes—ABS Recycling
operates the main sorting infrastructure in Chișinău, but domestic processing capacity for most plastic fractions
remains limited, and a significant portion of the collected plastic is exported to neighbouring countries,
particularly Romania, for downstream recycling.
An analysis of the current situation shows that, although the regulatory framework establishes obligations
regarding the achievement of recycling and recovery targets for packaging waste, the Republic of Moldova
currently lacks sufficient authorized domestic treatment and recycling capacity for all material streams.
Particularly in the case of plastics and metals, the recycling stage takes place predominantly outside the
country, through the export of waste to authorized facilities in Romania and other jurisdictions. Recycling
operators provide information on the resulting fractions and the quantities actually treated, data that is
subsequently used in the reporting process to the Environmental Agency.
Under these circumstances, the concept of “recycling” at the national level must be interpreted broadly, as part
of an operational chain that includes collection and sorting within the country, as well as subsequent treatment
abroad. However, reliance on exports for downstream processing creates a number of structural limitations.
First, reliance on exports entails additional logistics costs and exposes the system to operational risks,
including transport restrictions or changes in regulations governing cross-border waste shipments. Second,
the lack of domestic processing capacity for key packaging fractions limits the development of a domestic
circular economy, as the recovery of secondary raw materials takes place outside the country, without
generating direct economic benefits locally. Third, this model reduces control over the final stages of the
recycling chain, including verification of actual recycling and reported recovery performance against
established targets.
In addition, the national regulatory framework requires EPR collective systems to enter into contracts with
authorized waste treatment operators. In the absence of a sufficient number of authorized operators at the
national level for certain packaging fractions, this requirement is met by contracting external operators, which
increases dependence on external recycling markets.
Sorting and preparation for recycling—the physical separation of packaging waste into clean, marketable
material fractions—is carried out within the country to a limited extent, primarily through the sorting facility
operated by ABS Recycling in Chișinău and a small number of other authorized operators. However, the
sorting infrastructure outside the capital is in its infancy, and the quality of the sorted fractions is affected by
                                                                                                                  55
Study on Packaging Waste Management in the Republic of Moldova




low rates of source separation at the household level and the mixing of packaging waste with residual municipal
waste in collection systems.
The current system can be characterized as one based on partial in-house sorting and outsourced downstream
recycling, a model that is broadly consistent with how smaller countries in the region manage packaging waste
during the initial stages of EPR system development. The recycling of more complex or contaminated
packaging fractions—multi-layer packaging, certain types of plastic, composite materials—is capital-intensive
and requires significant economies of scale, which the Republic of Moldova cannot yet achieve domestically.
However, the structural deficiency of the packaging waste management system in the Republic of Moldova is
not primarily related to recycling capacity, but rather to the coverage of collection, source separation, data
quality, and the governance of the EPR system. Increasing the volumes of separately collected packaging
waste, improving sorting infrastructure, and strengthening reporting and verification systems are prerequisites
for any significant expansion of domestic recycling capacity. For material fractions for which there is already
domestic demand—paper, cardboard, glass, and metals—the priority is to improve collection rates and
material quality. For plastics, a longer-term strategy will be needed to develop domestic reprocessing capacity,
with a view to reducing dependence on exports and supporting Moldova’s transition to a circular economy.
4.3.4.   Waste disposal and its implications for packaging waste management

Moldova’s waste disposal infrastructure remains one of the most critical vulnerabilities of the national waste
management system. The majority of municipal solid waste collected nationwide is directed to disposal sites
that, for the most part, do not comply with EU standards. These sites lack basic operational and environmental
protection measures—including leachate collection systems, landfill gas capture infrastructure, and
appropriate cover materials—and a significant number of them operate without permits or environmental
authorizations. Uncontrolled waste dumps, many of which have been formally closed but continue to receive
waste in practice, remain widespread, particularly in rural areas.
A defining characteristic of disposal sites in the Republic of Moldova is the near-total absence of truck scales
and measurement infrastructure. Waste arriving at these sites is not weighed, and quantities are estimated
based on the number of vehicles and assumed load volumes—a methodology that is inherently imprecise and
prone to systematic overestimation, given that vehicles are not always fully loaded, and the degree of waste
compaction varies. This means that landfill operators, as well as the public authorities responsible for oversight,
do not have a reliable picture of the quantities of waste deposited, its composition, or the evolution of disposal
volumes over time.
The management of disposal sites is further hampered by limited operational capacity. Many of these sites are
managed by local government authorities or small operators, who lack the technical expertise, equipment, and
financial resources necessary to maintain even basic operational standards. Records of waste quantities and
types are inadequate, inspections are infrequent, and enforcement of existing requirements is limited. The co-
disposal of municipal solid waste alongside agricultural waste, construction waste, and industrial waste at the
same sites is common, which further distorts data on waste composition and quantities.
For packaging waste, this situation has significant implications. In the absence of functional management and
measurement systems at disposal sites, it is not possible to determine what proportion of packaging waste
ends up in landfills, whether separate collection efforts result in a real diversion from disposal, or whether
recycling targets are actually met. Improving governance at disposal sites—through investments in weighing
infrastructure, stricter operational standards, enhanced monitoring, and the progressive closure and
rehabilitation of non-compliant sites—is therefore not only an environmental imperative but also a fundamental
prerequisite for any credible packaging waste management system and extended producer responsibility.

4.4. Gaps in the collection and recycling of packaging waste
4.4.1.   Infrastructure deficiencies

The fundamental challenge facing the collection and recycling of packaging waste in the Republic of Moldova
is not primarily a regulatory one, but a physical one. The legal framework establishing EPR obligations and
collection and recovery requirements is, to a large extent, in place. What is missing is the infrastructure needed
to give these obligations practical meaning: containers, vehicles, sorting facilities, and transfer stations that
would allow separately collected packaging waste to be transported from households to recyclers throughout
the country.
The current infrastructure deficit manifests differently across the country. In Chișinău, there is a partial separate
collection system—color-coded containers installed by sanitation operators and collective EPR systems, a
functional sorting station operated by ABS Recycling, and collection services covering most of the urban
population. The system is incomplete and underperforms, but its basic components are in place. Outside the
                                                                                                                  56
Study on Packaging Waste Management in the Republic of Moldova




capital, the situation is deteriorating rapidly. In district capitals and larger cities, the separate collection
infrastructure is limited to isolated container points located in central areas, without systematic coverage of
residential neighbourhoods or surrounding localities. In small towns and peri-urban areas, separate collection
is largely absent. In rural areas—where approximately half of Moldova’s population lives—organized waste
collection of any kind covers only a small portion of households, and source-separated collection of packaging
is practically non-existent.
This geographical concentration of infrastructure represents the most significant obstacle to achieving
packaging waste recycling targets aligned with EU requirements. Recycling rates cannot increase without
collection, and collection cannot function without physical infrastructure that is accessible to waste generators.
Currently, there is a significant gap between areas where infrastructure exists and areas where the population
lives.
4.4.2.   The regionalization program as the main driver of change

The most significant opportunity to reduce the infrastructure deficit is the regionalization program implemented
through the “Solid Waste in Moldova” project in the eight waste management zones. This program—the largest
structural investment in the waste management sector in the Republic of Moldova—creates the basic
infrastructure for a national waste management system: regional transfer stations, sorting facilities, and
collection infrastructure to serve both urban and rural areas.
The regionalization model provides for each household to receive two containers—one for recyclable waste
and one for residual waste—with the materials to be transported to transfer stations, where recyclable fractions
will be sorted and sent for recycling. If implemented on the planned scale, this model would, for the first time,
create a physical route through which separately collected packaging waste could reach recycling facilities
across the entire country, including rural areas that are currently not covered by any organized collection
service.
Progress across the eight zones is uneven. Region 5—Ungheni, Nisporeni, Călărași—is the most advanced,
with procurement procedures for infrastructure already underway. Feasibility studies are currently being
developed or have recently been completed for Regions 4 and 7. Regions 1 and 2 are in earlier stages of
institutional and technical preparation. The timeline for full national coverage extends through the end of this
decade, and delays have already prompted the Government to declare the project a public utility of national
interest in order to accelerate authorization and land access procedures.
For packaging waste, the regionalization program offers an already established platform into which permanent
separate collection infrastructure can be integrated—provided that the design of transfer stations, collection
routes, and the distribution of containers to households explicitly takes into account packaging fractions and
their subsequent routing to sorting and recycling facilities. The risk is that regional infrastructure will be
designed primarily around residual waste management—diversion from landfilling, transfer, and disposal—
without adequately integrating the separate collection and sorting requirements applicable to packaging and
other streams regulated by the EPR. Ensuring the integration of requirements for the management of
packaging waste from the initial stage of regional infrastructure design, rather than through subsequent
adjustments, is one of the most important short-term decisions for this sector.
4.4.3.   Sorting capacity and the “missing intermediate segment”

Even where separate collection of packaging waste exists or is under development, the lack of sorting
infrastructure outside the municipality of Chișinău creates a critical bottleneck. Separately collected packaging
fractions—paper, plastic, glass, and metal—must be sorted into clean, marketable material streams before
they can be delivered to recyclers. In the absence of sorting capacity, separately collected materials either
accumulate with no clear destination or are reintroduced into the residual waste stream, negating the
investments made in collection.
The sorting station operated by ABS Recycling in Chișinău is the only significant facility of its kind in the
country. The regional transfer stations developed under the “Solid Waste” project will include basic sorting
lines, but their capacity, design specifications, and level of operational readiness relative to the projected
volumes of packaging waste remain to be confirmed. Meanwhile, the lack of sorting infrastructure in most of
the country means that, even if collection coverage were to expand rapidly, the recovered materials would
have no functional destination.
Closing this gap requires a coordinated investment plan for sorting infrastructure, aligned with the
implementation timeline of the regionalization program and the projected volumes of packaging waste for each
zone. Given the small size of individual waste management zones and the capital-intensive nature of modern
sorting equipment, inter-municipal and inter-zonal cooperation in developing sorting infrastructure—through

shared facilities serving multiple districts—will most likely represent the most economically viable model for
most of the country, outside the municipality of Chișinău.
4.4.4.   Recycling capacity and dependence on exports

Downstream of the sorting stage, Moldova’s domestic recycling capacity adequately covers certain packaging
material streams—paper and cardboard through Candelux-Com and, to an increasing extent, glass, through
the new facility opened in Chișinău in 2025—but remains insufficient for plastics and limited for metals.
Particularly in the case of plastic packaging, most sorted materials are exported for recycling, mainly to
Romania.
Export-based recycling is a rational response to the constraints of a small market and will remain a necessary
component of the system for the foreseeable future. The priority is not to prematurely replace exports with
domestic processing, but to ensure that exported streams are properly certified, traceable, and counted toward
national recycling targets—as well as to progressively create the conditions for developing domestic
reprocessing capacity as collection volumes increase.
For material streams for which there is already domestic demand for recycling—paper, glass, and metals—
the main constraint is not recycling capacity, but the volume collected and the quality of the material. Improving
source separation practices, reducing contamination in separately collected fractions, and expanding collection
service coverage are the levers most likely to increase, in the short term, the quantities of packaging waste
reaching domestic recyclers.
For plastics, justifying a longer-term investment in domestic reprocessing capacity will depend on achieving
collection volumes that make processing economically viable at scale. Given the size of Moldova’s market,
this may ultimately require a regional approach—by positioning Moldova within broader regional recycling
value chains, rather than attempting to develop fully autonomous domestic capacities for all plastic fractions.
4.4.5.   Priorities for the development of the physical system

Based on the previous analysis, the most important priorities for developing the physical infrastructure needed
for the collection and recycling of packaging waste in the Republic of Moldova are detailed below.
The immediate priority is to ensure that the regional infrastructure developed under the “Solid Waste in
Moldova” project is designed and equipped from the outset to manage separately collected packaging waste—
by providing an adequate number of household containers, sorting lines at transfer stations capable of
processing the main packaging fractions, and clearly defined routes for the subsequent routing of materials to
recycling facilities.
At the same time, the separate collection infrastructure in Chișinău and existing urban centres must be
strengthened and expanded—by increasing the density of containers in residential areas, replacing outdated
containers, and improving collection frequency, in order to reduce contamination and improve the quality of
collected materials.
For rural areas, developing a practical and financially sustainable collection model—whether through a regional
container distribution program, village-level consolidation points, or mobile collection services—represents the
most urgent unresolved challenge. Without a viable collection model in rural areas, packaging recycling targets
aligned with EU requirements will remain unattainable, regardless of the performance of the urban system.
Sorting capacity must be developed in parallel with the collection infrastructure, with investments aligned with
the implementation timeline of the regionalization program and designed for shared use at the intermunicipal
level where the volumes generated by individual areas do not justify independent facilities.
Finally, the domestic recycling market for packaging materials must be actively developed—through
transparent pricing mechanisms, long-term material take-back agreements between EPR collective systems
and recyclers, and conditions that make investments in domestic reprocessing capacity commercially attractive
as collected volumes increase.

5. Key conclusions and
recommendations
The analysis presented in this study reveals a picture more complex than mere implementation shortcomings.
Moldova’s packaging waste management system has a regulatory framework and a set of functional actors—
collective systems, authorized operators, a registration and reporting architecture—and these are real
achievements that provide a foundation on which to build. However, work is still needed on the legal framework
itself to align it with EU requirements: recycling targets set below the necessary levels, a regulatory framework
that does not yet reflect the new requirements of Regulation (EU) 2025/40 on packaging and packaging waste,
and a trajectory that, if left unchanged, will not bring Moldova into compliance with its EU accession obligations
by the deadline.
In addition to legal gaps, there are a number of structural governance and operational shortcomings—
regarding auditing and verification, governance of the collective systems market, coverage of separate
collection infrastructure, and the involvement of local authorities—which, taken together, mean that the system
is not functioning at the level required by current objectives.
Progress toward meeting collection and recycling targets is slow, and the proportion of producers evading their
EPR obligations remains high, a fact clearly highlighted by the discrepancy between the estimated quantity of
packaging placed on the market (POM)—based on packaging waste volumes—and the quantities reported by
EPR systems. Separate collection of packaging from municipal waste covers only a small portion of the territory
and population, being limited mainly to Chișinău and a few urban centres. At the same time, a significant
portion of recyclable packaging materials is managed by the informal collection sector and is not accounted
for in official reports.

The conclusions and recommendations in the following sections are organized around three themes, which
reflect the structure of this report: (1) the legal and regulatory framework, (2) the institutional and governance
architecture, and (3) the physical management of packaging waste. Within each thematic area, the most urgent
issues are presented first, distinguishing between proposals to amend or supplement legislation, which require
legislative action; governance improvements, which require institutional efforts; and operational priorities,
which require investment and coordination. The recommendations aim to guide policymakers toward concrete
and sequential actions, based on the premise that, without legislative realignment, the rest of the system
cannot align with current EU requirements, regardless of how well it is governed in other respects.

5.1. Policy and regulatory framework
Moldova’s regulatory framework on packaging and packaging waste is, for the most part, transposed from
relevant EU legislation and is generally fit for purpose as a starting point. The most pressing issues are not
major revisions, but targeted corrections and clarifications that would eliminate ambiguities, remedy
transposition deficiencies, and prepare for alignment with Regulation (EU) 2025/40 on packaging and
packaging waste, which already constitutes the mandatory EU reference framework against which Moldova’s
alignment will be assessed in the accession process.
Addressing transposition gaps regarding recycling targets and calculation methodology. National
recycling targets for packaging are established in legislation, but the calculation methodology and reporting
                                                                                                                  59
Study on Packaging Waste Management in the Republic of Moldova




procedures are not fully aligned with the requirements of Directive 94/62/EC and its implementing acts.
Revising the calculation methodology and aligning the targets with EU requirements is a priority legislative
action, even more so as the new Regulation (EU) 2025/40 introduces more ambitious targets, recycled content
requirements, and reuse obligations that will require a substantial adaptation of the national framework.
Alignment of the regulatory framework with Regulation (EU) 2025/40 on packaging and packaging
waste. The new European Regulation introduces additional requirements compared to the old directive:
obligations regarding the minimum recycled content per packaging category, reuse and refill targets, design
requirements for recyclability, restrictions on certain packaging formats, and significantly more detailed EPR
governance. Preparing for the adoption of this framework is a medium-term legislative priority, but one that
must begin immediately, given that some requirements will apply shortly after accession.
The restriction on the right to collect packaging waste and its market-level consequences. Pursuant to
Article 12, paragraph (18) of Law No. 209/2016, the collection of waste from products subject to EPR
regulations is limited to collective systems and the operators contracted by them. This restriction, analysed in
detail in the chapter on the institutional framework, limits competition, reduces the operational flexibility of
collective systems, and may restrict independent operators’ access to collection activities. A more flexible
approach—allowing independent operators to collect packaging waste provided they meet defined standards
and deliver the materials to authorized facilities—would increase the number of available operators, reduce
dependence on the capacity of collective systems, and facilitate the gradual formalization of the informal
sector.
Eliminating the risk of double penalties for the same quantities of unrecycled waste. The current
regulatory framework creates, through the combination of several legal provisions, a situation in which the
same shortfall in recovery relative to the target can generate two distinct payment obligations—the packaging
fee provided for by Law No. 1540/1998 and the operational management costs imposed by Law No. 209/2016.
This overlap creates a disproportionate and discriminatory economic burden that is not justified by the
environmental impact and should be remedied by clarifying and harmonizing the two financial liability
mechanisms.
Introduction of EPR requirements for single-use plastic (SUP) products. Directive (EU) 2019/904 on
single-use plastics introduces specific EPR obligations for certain categories of SUP products—including
infrastructure for the collection of litter, cleanliness of public spaces, requirements regarding recycled content,
and awareness-raising measures—which have not yet been fully transposed or integrated into the national
legal framework. The transposition of these requirements is a legal obligation and a priority in the context of
the accession process.
Introduction of eco-modulation of EPR financial contributions. Law No. 209/2016 mentions eco-
modulation in principle, but the Packaging Regulation does not implement it. The new Regulation (EU) 2025/40
introduces eco-modulation as a mandatory element of EPR systems for packaging. Preparations for the
implementation of eco-modulation—including the definition of relevant criteria, calculation methodology, and
reporting requirements that allow for the differentiation of packaging based on its environmental performance—
should be made, as implementation will require both legislative changes and a significantly higher granularity
of reporting data than is currently available.

5.2. Institutional framework and EPR governance
The institutional architecture for implementing the EPR in Moldova is broadly defined but poorly
operationalized. The Environmental Agency has the appropriate mandate but lacks the capacity and tools
necessary to exercise it effectively. Relationships among key actors—collective systems, local authorities,
producers, and sorting and recycling operators—are legally defined but insufficiently specified in practice. The
result is a system that operates based on goodwill and bilateral agreements rather than clear and enforceable
rules.
5.2.1.   Governance of EPR systems and market structure

Addressing the non-profit status of collective systems. The requirement that collective systems operate
as non-profit organizations reflects a legitimate policy intent but creates practical constraints regarding long-
term infrastructure investments, financial planning, and access to financing. Instead of changing the legal
form—which would be disruptive at a stage when stakeholders are already established and contracts are in
force—the Ministry of the Environment could issue specific guidelines clarifying how collective systems can
legitimately invest in infrastructure, build up reserves, and structure long-term contracts within the non-
commercial framework.
Addressing challenges related to cost transparency within multi-stream EPR systems. Several
collective systems hold simultaneous authorizations for multiple EPR waste streams. While this can generate
                                                                                                                60
Study on Packaging Waste Management in the Republic of Moldova




operational efficiency, it creates challenges for cost transparency and data traceability. A pragmatic first step
would be to develop guidelines on cost allocation methodologies for collective EPR organizations managing
multiple streams. Over time, stream-specific reporting could be progressively incorporated into annual
reporting requirements and the audit framework.
The need to consolidate collective systems. The number of authorized collective systems for packaging is
disproportionate to the size of the market, an issue identified by stakeholders as a major contributor to
fragmentation, operational inefficiency, and administrative complexity. EU best practices in comparable small
Member States have evolved toward a limited number of systems for each waste stream, with clearly defined
obligations and strict oversight. Regulatory conditions, such as minimum market share thresholds or minimum
operational requirements as a precondition for authorization, could encourage gradual consolidation without
limiting the producer’s ability to choose between different compliance systems.
Establishment of a “clearing house” mechanism. There is no central mechanism for coordination among
collective systems, for verifying market share declarations, for allocating collection obligations, or for cross-
checking performance reported at the system level. The new Regulation (EU) 2025/40 on packaging explicitly
requires Member States to establish such a mechanism, defined as “an independent third party tasked with
overseeing that organizations implementing extended producer responsibility fulfil their EPR obligations in a
coordinated manner” (Art. 46, para. (2)). Establishing this mechanism is, therefore, not merely a best practice,
but a direct future obligation arising from the EU accession process. It must: maintain a single register of
producers, linked to customs and tax data; reconcile fee payments and collection obligations among EPR
organizations; verify the consistency of reported performance; publish annual aggregate data at the system
level; and establish shared responsibility for national coverage among competing EPR organizations.
5.2.2.   Cooperation with local authorities and collection standards

Adoption of a standard cooperation agreement between collective systems and local public
authorities. The model agreement provided for by legislation has not been adopted, which means that this
cooperation depends on bilateral negotiations leading to inconsistent agreements—or a lack thereof—at the
national level. Competent authorities should adopt a standard contractual framework detailing minimum
content: financing agreements, service standards, geographic coverage obligations, reporting and verification
requirements, as well as dispute resolution mechanisms. Financing agreements should account for the cost
difference between urban and rural collection. In the case of packaging, the standard contract is of particular
importance compared to other EPR streams, as the municipal waste management service is the primary—
rather than a complementary—channel for the collection of household packaging waste.
Inclusion of minimum requirements for the collection system in the authorizations granted to collective
systems. The current authorization framework does not establish measurable standards for geographic
coverage, the density of separate collection points, or household accessibility. Without these requirements,
EPR organizations can fulfil their obligations in a manner largely defined by themselves. Requiring specific
and measurable operational commitments as a precondition for authorization is the most direct lever available
to prevent the concentration of collection on easily accessible commercial streams at the expense of collection
from households.
5.2.3.   Verification, audit, and data integrity

Implementation of a comprehensive audit trail. The current system does not provide for meaningful
independent verification at any stage: producers self-report the quantities of packaging placed on the market
to the collective system, which in turn aggregates them and reports them to the Environmental Agency, and
the Agency publishes the results without independent verification. A functional audit trail should cross-
reference producers’ declarations with accounting and tax records—similar to the VAT system—enabling
cross-checking with the State Tax Service. The annual reports of collective systems should be subject to a
mandatory independent financial and operational audit prior to submission. The Environmental Agency must
continue to cross-check data on system performance with import data from the Customs Service.
A prerequisite for this is the development of practical guidelines for obligated businesses on how to document
and report the quantities of packaging placed on the market—specifying which accounting records should
serve as the basis for calculating EPR financial contributions, how quantities for different types of packaging
should be calculated, and how declarations should be reconciled with import and sales data.
The annual reports of EPR systems should be published in an accessible format—a minor amendment to the
applicable secondary legislation that would significantly improve accountability to producers, municipalities,
and civil society.
Verification of data from waste management operators and local public authorities. In the case of
packaging waste, local public authorities and waste management operators are not a complementary collection

5.2.4.   Institutional capacity and stakeholder engagement

Strengthening the Environmental Agency’s oversight capacity. In nearly all aspects of EPR
implementation—system authorization, producer registration, data verification, monitoring, and enforcement
of penalties—the Agency’s current capacity is insufficient. Strengthening this capacity requires additional staff,
formalized protocols for data sharing with the Customs Service and the State Tax Service, investments in the
reliability and analytical functionality of the SIA MD, as well as the development of internal guidelines and
training on EPR oversight functions. In the context of packaging, this challenge is particularly acute: the number
of obligated producers is far greater than for WEEE or batteries, encompassing virtually any importer or
producer of packaged consumer goods, which makes monitoring compliance and identifying unregistered
producers significantly more complex.
Establishing a mechanism for structured dialogue with industry. Industry involvement in EPR governance
for packaging is substantial, but often reactive and driven by (potential) disputes—particularly in the context of
the SDA administrator designation process, frequent changes to secondary legislation, and uncertainties
regarding the application of EPR obligations. A permanent consultative body—or at least a structured annual
review process involving the Environmental Agency, the Ministry of the Environment, collective systems,
producer associations, and sorting and recycling operators—would provide a predictable channel through
which implementation difficulties could be identified early and gaps in guidance addressed proactively.
5.2.5.   Collection, sorting, recycling, and export

The physical system for the separate collection and recovery of packaging waste in Moldova is the area where
the gap between legal obligations and on-the-ground reality is most visible. Separate collection of packaging
covers a small fraction of the territory and population, is concentrated mainly in Chișinău, remains financially
fragile and depends on donor funding, inadequately equipped municipal sanitation operators, and the informal
sector, rather than on a sustainable system financed through the EPR. Sorting capacity is insufficient at the
national level, and the recycling of more complex or less valuable materials remains dependent on export.
Develop a national operational plan for the separate collection of packaging. Moldova has legal
obligations, collective systems, and some collection infrastructure, but lacks a national vision for what the
separate packaging collection system should look like, how many collection points are needed and where,
which actors are responsible for which obligations, and how the system will be sustainably financed. An
operational plan—developed by the Environmental Agency in consultation with collective systems, local
authorities, and the Ministry of the Environment—should define: the minimum density of collection points based
on the size of the locality and the type of packaging; the integration of separate packaging collection into the
regional waste management infrastructure; the financing model linking EPR financial contributions to
investments in collection infrastructure; and the monitoring framework for verifying coverage and performance.
Testing proposed collection systems through pilot projects before nationwide implementation. Before
committing to a specific model for separate packaging collection on a national scale—whether it involves
collection at street-side collection points by material type, a street-container collection system, or combinations
thereof — Moldova could use the initial phases of regional infrastructure development as an opportunity to test
and compare different approaches in various types of settlements. Pilot projects should test container density,
the number of separately collected fractions, communication approaches, collection frequency, and the
response of different population groups—urban versus rural, households versus the commercial sector.
Regional waste management zones should become an important pillar of national coverage with
separate collection services. The eight waste management zones currently under development represent
an important opportunity for Moldova to achieve national coverage for the separate collection of packaging
waste. Once operational, transfer stations and street collection points, as well as door-to-door collection
systems, can serve as permanent collection points for recyclable packaging fractions, provided that this
function is planned from the outset in their design. At the same time, the regional sorting capacities to be
developed will be essential for processing separately collected materials. Still, the EPR system should be
taking full cost coverage and responsibility for the development of these systems. The large scale infrastructure
development of the integrated systems may take time and may be developing at different pace in different
regions. Coordination between public and private actors is needed, investing in key infrastructure development

Addressing the sorting capacity deficit. One of the structural gaps specific to the packaging sector is the
lack of sufficient sorting capacities at the national level to process the quantities of separately collected
packaging that would result from a functional collection system. Without proper sorting, separately collected
materials cannot enter the recycling stream at the required quality. Planning regional sorting capacities—in
coordination with regional waste management infrastructure—is an operational priority that must be addressed
in parallel with the expansion of separate collection.
Investments in communication and public awareness as a system-level obligation. Citizen participation
in the separate collection of packaging remains low, affecting both the quantity and quality of recyclable
materials entering the formal system. EPR organizations should be required to allocate a defined portion of
their operating budgets to awareness campaigns and to report in detail on their coverage, methodology, and
impact. The Environmental Agency should develop a national communication strategy for separate collection,
providing a common visual identity and a minimum communication framework.
Formalizing the role of the informal sector in packaging collection. Informal packaging collectors—street
vendors and recyclable material dealers who collect paper, cardboard, plastic, and metals directly from
households and businesses—already operate in a manner similar to that of the formal recycling sector. This
characteristic makes them easier to integrate into the formal system. The creation of specific mechanisms—
framework contracts between collective systems and small collectors, pay-per-ton schemes, minimum
documentation requirements, a digital traceability system using one of the existing dedicated applications—
could progressively formalize this sector, increase officially reported volumes, expand geographic coverage in
rural areas, and contribute to meeting recycling targets at a low marginal cost.
Management of the deposit system (SDA) as a central element of beverage packaging collection. The
appointment of the SDA administrator in May 2026 and the operationalization deadline set for January 1, 2027,
create significant time pressure for the system’s launch. The interaction between the DRS and the EPR system
for packaging—particularly regarding the allocation of financial and operational responsibilities among the DRS
administrator, collective systems, waste management operators, and local authorities—needs to be clarified.
European experience shows that well-designed deposit systems can achieve very high collection rates for the
targeted beverage packaging, contributing significantly to meeting national recycling targets.
Strategic engagement in regional recycling value chains. Moldova is already integrated into regional
recycling markets for certain materials, but largely on an ad-hoc basis. Collective systems and operators
should be encouraged to develop long-term contractual relationships with regional reception facilities—
especially for materials for which domestic recycling capacities are insufficient—providing price predictability
and volume guarantees to support investments in domestic sorting and pre-recycling capacities.

5.3. Final remarks
Moldova’s EPR system for packaging and packaging waste has a more solid foundation than an analysis of
its shortcomings might suggest. The legal framework is largely in place, collective systems are functioning,
and there is some separate collection infrastructure. The informal packaging recycling sector captures
significant volumes of recyclable material. The gap between the current situation and a functional system lies
in governance, coordination, investment, and the political will to consistently enforce obligations.
The recommendations in this chapter are presented in a deliberate order. Legislative alignments can be
achieved relatively quickly. Governance improvements—namely the “clearing house” mechanism, the audit
chain, the model contract with local public authorities, and public reporting—require institutional effort but do
not entail significant financial investments. Infrastructure improvements, sorting capacities, separate collection
in rural areas, integration into regional waste management zones, and the operationalization of the SDA
require both investment and coordination, but the regional waste management program already underway
provides the framework through which many of these can be achieved.
The system needs clearer rules, better enforcement of existing rules, and a national plan that tells all
stakeholders—collective systems, local authorities, producers, sorting and recycling operators, and citizens—
exactly what is expected of them and the timeline for action.
